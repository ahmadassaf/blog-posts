I open source my work and write this blog partly because I want other people to be able to inspect what I'm doing. I also share on Medium or Substack and post to Hacker News. A repo, an article, or a question gives someone something they can respond to, including the parts I haven't figured out yet.

I can reread my own notes and miss the same flaw. Sharing them gives someone else a chance to point out an edge case, link prior work I didn't know about, or ask a question I can't answer. None of that is guaranteed by pressing publish. Sometimes nobody responds. But making the work available creates the possibility.

Open source gives someone a way to run the code and test the choices I've made. A blog post gives me room to explain the reasoning behind those choices. Sharing on other platforms gives that work a chance to reach people who wouldn't have found this site. Someone can learn from it without ever getting in touch, which is a reason to make it available even when there is no conversation afterward.

## Giving someone enough to respond

An unfinished piece of work still needs enough context to be useful. A half-working repo is easier to help with if someone can tell what it is meant to do and where it fails. A question gets more specific answers when it includes what has already been tried. Otherwise, the person responding has to reconstruct the problem before they can say anything about it.

There is a difference between leaving the answer open and leaving the reader to guess the question. A short note can explain the problem, show the result so far, and name the uncertainty without pretending to be a complete guide. That uncertainty gives someone a place to contribute.

For example, a note about a fix might explain that it works for the input that originally failed, while another case is still untested. Someone can then help examine that limit. If the note simply declares the problem solved, both the writer and the reader have less reason to look for it.

## You need a thick skin

There are also a lot of mean people online. Sharing publicly means accepting that someone may dismiss the work, question your competence, or turn a technical disagreement into a personal remark. Putting effort into something doesn't guarantee a fair response, or even that the person responding has read it carefully. That is part of what makes publishing unfinished work uncomfortable.

An abrasive comment can still contain a valid correction, and a polite one can be wrong. A bug report with a reproducible example gives me something to investigate. An insult on its own gives me nothing to fix. Treating all criticism as hostility would defeat part of the reason I shared the work in the first place.

It gets harder when the useful criticism and the insult arrive in the same comment. I can take the technical point seriously without agreeing with the personal judgment attached to it. Fixing the work doesn't require replying to every provocation, and leaving an argument doesn't make the other person's judgment correct.

I try to make clear what I know, what I've tried, and what I'm still unsure about. That gives people a fairer basis for responding, though it won't make everybody kind. Polished work can stay open to correction too; being willing to revise it matters more than defending every choice I made when I published it.

## Choosing an audience

It doesn't have to mean publishing to everyone. A conversation with a trusted colleague can do the same job. Private notes accumulate too, and some ideas need quiet time before an audience is useful. The question is who could help me see something I'm currently missing.

That also puts a limit on what belongs in public. Someone else's information is not mine to share just because it appeared in a useful learning experience. A note can explain the general problem without including private context, or stay with the people who are already involved.

Shawn Wang's [Learn in Public](https://swyx.io/learn-in-public) makes a good case for sharing small pieces as you go. His companion [Learn in Private](https://swyx.io/learn-in-private) is worth reading alongside it. I like having both in view: sharing is a useful practice, not an obligation to turn every bit of learning into content.

## Keeping the correction with the work

A public note also gives me something to return to when I've forgotten the details myself.

That usefulness depends on what happens after publication too. If a reply reveals that the explanation is wrong, the original note needs the correction. Leaving it only in the conversation means a later reader can still follow the old advice. A brief update explaining what changed also preserves the reasoning: the first attempt becomes something to learn from without remaining an unqualified recommendation.

A note on a problem you just solved is enough to start. So is a specific question. You don't need to wait until you can write the definitive guide, though you do owe the reader enough context to understand what you're sharing.
