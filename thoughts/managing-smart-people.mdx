The smartest people I've worked with don't need to be told what to do. They need context, autonomy, and trust.

That still leaves a manager with plenty to do. Someone needs to explain why a problem matters, which constraints are fixed, and where the engineer can make the decision. Being good at solving a problem doesn't make you able to infer a customer promise that nobody told you about. Without that context, someone can do excellent work on a problem the business no longer needs solved.

Brilliant people also attract requests. Every hard question, every escalation, every "quick look" gets routed to them, and they bounce from demand to demand like a pinball in an organizational machine. Each request is reasonable on its own. Together, they can consume the time that was supposed to go toward the difficult work only that person has enough context to finish.

Protecting that time means making choices other people can see. If an engineer is responsible for a difficult migration, their manager needs to help decide which requests can wait and who will handle the rest. Telling someone to focus while continuing to send them every urgent question leaves the conflict for them to resolve. They may end up working longer simply to do both jobs.

## Enough context to disagree

Giving someone a problem leaves room for them to find a better solution than the one I had in mind. It also leaves room for them to question the problem. A request to make a slow report faster might turn into a conversation about whether the report needs to run at all. That is useful disagreement, provided we understand who depends on it and what would happen if it disappeared.

The constraints need the same scrutiny. A deadline tied to an external commitment is different from a date chosen because it looked tidy on a plan. If those are presented with equal force, the engineer has to guess where there is room to negotiate. Being explicit gives them a chance to propose a smaller scope or a different sequence of work.

Autonomy also needs a way to check that the work is still heading somewhere useful. Agreeing when to review a proposal or test an assumption makes it easier to leave someone alone between those points. Waiting until the end to reveal that their approach was unacceptable wastes the freedom they were given.

## When to step in

Sometimes the useful intervention is a question: which assumption is holding up the decision? Sometimes it is an introduction to someone who has solved a similar problem. If the engineer is waiting for another team, the manager may need to resolve that dependency. Asking for more progress updates won't remove it.

There is a separate responsibility to the rest of the team. If every difficult decision needs the same person's approval, their expertise has become a dependency. Making space for pairing or a written explanation gives other people a chance to learn. That work takes time too, so it needs to be accounted for when deciding what the engineer can deliver.

The best compliment I've received as a manager: "You helped me do the best work of my career." I take that as a useful standard for the job. It asks me to look at what someone was able to do with my help, including whether they had the time and support to finish it.
