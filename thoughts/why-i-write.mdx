Writing clarifies thinking.

I can hold an idea in my head and think I understand it. But when I try to write it down, I discover the gaps in my understanding. The act of converting thoughts to words forces precision. An idea that felt complete can turn out to depend on a word I haven't defined, or on an assumption I haven't examined.

It is easy to think that a design is too complicated. Explaining what makes it complicated takes more work. Is the problem the number of concepts someone has to learn? The way a change in one place affects another? Or the fact that nobody can explain why a particular branch exists? Those are different problems. Writing the complaint down gives me a chance to distinguish them before I reach for a solution.

That is what I mean when I say I write to discover what I think. I can start with a position and find that the reasons for it don't quite hold together. The useful part of the draft may be the sentence I can't finish.

## Giving an idea enough detail

A private note can stop at the point where it has helped me understand something. It doesn't need an introduction or an ending that sounds conclusive. Sometimes the useful result is a narrower question, with enough context that I can return to it later.

For example, a note saying that an approach didn't work leaves too much out. Recording what it was supposed to do, and what happened instead, makes the note useful even if the next approach is still unclear. Explaining which expectation was wrong also keeps a failed attempt from becoming a vague impression that the whole idea is impossible.

This is why I write notes that no one else will read, and why I explain concepts to myself in documentation. Having to connect one sentence to the next exposes jumps that are easy to make in my head. If the conclusion depends on something I haven't explained, the reader will have to supply it. In a private note, that reader may just be me a few months later.

## Reading it back

Writing something clearly doesn't establish that it is true. A tidy explanation can still rest on a bad assumption. What it gives me is an argument I can inspect: I can see where an observation ends and an interpretation begins, and ask what would change the conclusion.

Reading a draft back is part of that inspection. A paragraph may repeat the one above because I haven't worked out how to move the argument forward. A confident opening may promise more than the rest can support. Revision gives me a way to resolve those problems while the cost is still a few sentences.

I maintain this blog even though my audience is small because the work of making an idea understandable is useful to me. Publishing adds a reader's needs to the process: background I took for granted has to be explained, and uncertainty has to be visible enough that someone can disagree with the right part.

I try to write every day, a little bit, even if it's just a thought. Keeping the practice small leaves room for notes that are unfinished or only useful to me. Some will develop into something I want to share; others have already done their job by helping me understand a problem.

When the writing is ready to leave the drawer, that's a different practice: [learning in public](/blog/category/thoughts/learning-in-public).
