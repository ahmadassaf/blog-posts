A little over a year ago my title changed. I had spent a decade at Beamery, first as a founding engineer and later building its AI and research team. By then, my work was research, pipelines, models and the occasional long argument about what “done” meant. Then a few of us started something new, and I became the CTO.

On the first day the word meant one thing to me: when it breaks, I'm the person responsible. I didn't yet know how many different things “it” would turn out to be.

## Doing it again, with people I already trusted

[Ten years at Beamery](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn) hadn't put me off startups. At Mav9, I liked the founding team, we had funding to hire, and design partners were waiting for us. I still wanted to build something from scratch. Now we had to build something those people could use.

My first decision as CTO was who to build it with. Most of the engineers I brought in had been on my team before. I knew how they worked, what they were good at, and what they would say when they disagreed with me. I could give one of them a problem and turn my attention elsewhere, trusting that the work would move and that they would come back when it needed a decision. With a product to build and a long list of other things on my desk, I needed that.

We had worked together inside a company with hundreds of people, a product that already existed, and teams around us for everything we did not do. Now there was nothing around us. I had to think about whether the people I trusted were also right for this much earlier stage. Some of what had made them valuable before mattered less now. Some of what mattered now, they had never had to do.

What I wanted to keep was how much we enjoyed working together. These were people who would choose difficult work because they found it interesting. I knew that from working alongside them.

These people came because I asked them to. If the company does not work, part of that is mine.

I trusted them. I had done much less to make sure they knew what they could decide, what they needed from me, or where to find it when I was busy.

## A fortnight in the job

In one recent fortnight, I dealt with:

- A TLS certificate that existed, but in the wrong cloud account, so it could not be used for the thing we needed it for.
- A compliance check asking who approved a particular pull request, and why the answer was “nobody”.
- An intercompany invoice between two of our own entities.
- A payroll run and a VAT return.
- A forecast of how long our cloud credits would last if we kept spending the way we were.
- A vendor's data-processing terms, read twice.
- A dashboard I built that nobody but me had opened.
- Another conversation about whether we needed a product manager, and what we would be asking that person to fix.

The other founders are based in Germany, and I am the one in the UK. Setting up the UK entity fell to me. So did finding an office, choosing a pension provider, arranging health insurance, running payroll and submitting VAT returns. None of this had been part of my engineering work at Beamery. For the first few months, a surprising share of being the CTO was being the only founder in the country where the company was incorporated.

The rest was harder to account for. There was no job description, just a founding team, a product to ship, and a growing list of things that needed someone to pick them up. I picked up a lot of them. Sometimes that was necessary. Sometimes it was easier than working out who should own the problem after that afternoon.

When I had a choice, technical work was particularly easy to reach for. I could close a ticket and know I had done something. It was harder to tell whether I had given someone enough context, made a good hiring decision, or helped the team depend on me a little less. I'm still learning how much that preference shaped the job I ended up doing.

I came into this role from the research and machine-learning side. I had never run production infrastructure. I had never been the name on a security policy. I had never submitted a VAT return. I've now done all three, and I would like to write about it before I forget what it felt like.

## Why now

I want to keep [learning in public](/blog/category/thoughts/learning-in-public) while I can still remember why these decisions seemed reasonable at the time. Knowing what I know now makes it very easy to pretend I should have known it then.

Asking the engineers to write down how their parts of the system worked helped me recover a picture I had been losing. I've handed over the front end too. Some newer changes are still being put into practice. I want to write down what helped, what I've changed and what I still need to check.

It is uncomfortable to write about a conversation when the other person might read it. I'm leaving teammates' names out. What I want to examine is how I responded and what I could have done better.

## What the series covers

These are the problems I've run into and the changes that followed:

- [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real). I felt replaced when engineers picked up work I had left undone. Telling them what I was struggling with and asking for their help gave me a way back into the work.
- [Trust Is a System You Design](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design). I had left people guessing about decisions and authority. We wrote down Owner and Sponsor roles, with duties on both sides.
- [Ten Engineers, No Product Manager](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet). We still could not afford a PM. I drafted the product responsibilities we owed the team and the engineering habits we could improve ourselves.
- [We Kept Trying to Plan Everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year). We were on our third version of planning and the team was telling me it was confusing. We are now trying a fourth, with fewer things to track and clearer roles.
- [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature). AI helps our small team explore, build and iterate faster. It also helps me with the reading, preparation and repeatable work around the code.
- [Compliance at AI Speed](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-compliance-at-ai-speed). Hundreds of changes a month strained our approval process. We separated critical repositories from experiments, tried different ways to review and release, and built a change gate. Then I had to check whether our policies matched what we had actually put in place.
- [A Year In, the Job Looks Different](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much). Building the front end and setting up the company were part of getting started. As the team grew, I had to change how I shared context, made decisions and handed over work.
