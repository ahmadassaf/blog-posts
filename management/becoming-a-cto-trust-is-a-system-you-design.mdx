An engineer told me that our commitment to the new front end had never been formally communicated. I was surprised. I felt as though I had said it in every call for a month.

In the same fortnight, a new engineer asked whether he could assign himself the goals he was already, in practice, owning, or whether each one had to wait for the planning meeting.

One person could not tell whether a company decision was settled. The other could not tell which decisions were his to make. Both were missing information I thought they already had.

I had [brought people I already trusted](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-nobody-handed-me-a-manual), so they could run with work while I was not looking. For most of the first year I treated that as the whole arrangement. I trusted their judgement and assumed they knew where to use it. Those two conversations showed me how much I had left them to infer.

## They needed to be able to trust me too

When I said I trusted the team, I meant I believed they could work things out, make sound choices and tell me when they needed help. I had spent much less time asking what they needed to believe about me. Would I stand behind a decision I had given them the authority to make? Would I explain a changed priority? Could they tell me my plan was wrong without the conversation becoming about their attitude?

Some of us had years of working together to draw on. That history could help, but it did not explain who could decide what in this company. Someone joining the team should not have to infer their authority from how familiar they are with me. My title tells them who is responsible. They still need to see how I carry that responsibility.

Much of that is ordinary follow-through. If I promise an answer, I need to return with it, or say why I cannot yet give one and when I will come back. If someone raises a concern, they should be able to find out what happened to it, including when I decide not to act on it. A clear refusal leaves someone able to plan. An unanswered concern leaves them guessing whether I heard it at all.

## A decision needs to look decided

At my last company, much of the structure already existed: levels, review processes, who signed off on what, which meetings a decision went through. I had not had to design it from scratch. Here, I was relying on habits we had not yet established and boundaries I had not explained.

People filled the gaps by watching me. Sometimes I was involved in a decision; sometimes I was buried in payroll. My absence could mean that the decision was theirs, that I had not seen it, or that I had assumed it was already settled. There was no reliable way to tell.

For the front-end commitment, reliability meant making the decision available to the people building against it. They needed to know what was settled, what remained open and what a change would mean for their work. Keeping that in scattered conversations made them check with me again, even when they were perfectly capable of deciding for themselves.

I had already suggested that we record decisions where the work lives, and write up decisions made on calls that day. It was a proposal for the team to challenge. The front-end conversation showed why I wanted it, and how much I needed to change my own habits before asking anyone else to change theirs.

## Who decides what

The new engineer needed to know which decisions he could make without waiting for me.

I heard a line somewhere that has stayed with me:

> I own the context; you own the execution.

For me, that starts with making the purpose and constraints available. The person doing the work still needs to question them when something does not add up.

We later wrote the Owner and Sponsor roles into our agreement on how we work. Every goal has two people. An Owner works out how to deliver it and makes the implementation decisions inside what was agreed. A Sponsor explains why it matters, is the voice of the product and the customer, and explicitly approves any change to scope. They have to be different people. The Owner does not wait for me on a library choice or the order of their own work. They do go back to the Sponsor before the goal grows, and the two of them agree that it is ready before it ships.

We also need to name who has the final say in each area. If your goal requires a decision in someone else's area, it is on you to tell them early and get their approval before that decision is final or the affected work starts. That covers the things I had been carrying as an unwritten list in my head. The person to involve is whoever owns the area, which is sometimes me and often not.

This gave me a much clearer answer to the new engineer's question. An agreed goal was his to deliver; he did not need to wait for another planning meeting to take responsibility for it. A scope change belonged with the Sponsor, and a decision affecting another area belonged with that area's owner. We had put the answer somewhere he could find it without needing to catch me on a call.

## Ownership has two sides

Two German terms helped me describe the duties on both sides. *Holschuld* is the duty to fetch: what you need to do your job, you go and get. Clarity, a decision, context, access, an answer. If something essential is missing, ask before building on an assumption. *Bringschuld* is the duty to deliver: what others need from you, you bring without being asked. The product context, a draft, the current state of your goal, a demo, bad news early.

The new engineer's question was a Holschuld: he was fetching clarity about a responsibility he already had. I had also suggested moving ahead with a draft when nobody answered. That needs a boundary: an Owner can proceed with an implementation choice inside the agreed scope, but silence cannot supply a required scope, area or release approval.

The front-end commitment was a Bringschuld I had not met. The decision existed, and I had not brought it to the people building against it in a form they could find. Both duties are mine as much as anyone's. Asking for missing context does not relieve me of making it available.

## Ship, show, ask

We have been working through the same question in code review. The delivery model we are putting into practice uses Ship / Show / Ask to make clear when a change needs someone else before it lands.

**Ship.** An ordinary change inside your agreed goal. The model allows the author to merge after the automated checks pass and the review findings are resolved. The author still needs to understand the change and take responsibility for it.

**Show.** The same kind of change, with a reason to share the result: an implementation approach someone could reuse, or progress on a customer journey we had already agreed. You merge and tell people where to look. Showing is a Bringschuld; it helps others learn from the work and keep their picture current.

**Ask.** A second engineer before merging. This covers authentication, secrets, infrastructure, migrations, large changes, and changes to shared contracts or risks outside your goal. Changing an interface another goal depends on belongs here, even when showing the result afterwards would also be useful.

The release rule is separate: production requires an independent human approval. Where merging deploys to production, that approval must happen before the merge. Our [review and release model](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-compliance-at-ai-speed) lets us keep that requirement while giving the Owner room to merge ordinary changes. We are still putting the checks into the tooling; the written rules are what we are implementing, not a claim that every repository already enforces them.

## Making it real

Milestones and goals live in one project in the code host, each goal with its Owner in the assignee field and its Sponsor named. Keeping those records current gives people somewhere to find the state of work and see who can make the next decision. Our [planning meetings](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year) use that shared picture. People also need to know what to expect from me when they act on it.

I also need to be careful about what happens after a mistake. Before adding another approval step, I need to ask whether the problem needs a lasting check, or better context and a conversation. A new gate creates work every time someone passes through it. I want to understand the risk it addresses and why that ongoing cost is justified. Our release approvals and other required safeguards still apply; trust is not permission to bypass them.

When an engineer uses AI, my confidence in that person's judgement still comes from working with them and seeing how they respond to consequences. A convincing output from an assistant does not carry that history. The engineer still has to [understand and own the change](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

## Leaving decisions with their owners

In a retro, the team gave each other credit for owning work end to end. That was already happening before we wrote down the roles. I wanted to build on it and make sure people knew who to turn to when they did need help.

The engineer who questioned the front-end commitment was willing to challenge my account of what I had communicated. I want anyone in the team to be able to do that, regardless of how long we have worked together. How I respond will affect whether they raise the next concern. If I spend the conversation proving how many times I mentioned the decision, I have missed what the engineer was telling me: it still was not clear enough to act on.

The written roles give me something to come back to when I see a reasonable implementation decision I would have made differently. I can ask whether new information changes the scope, the risk, or a commitment we have already made. If it does, I owe the Owner an explanation of what changed and why it matters. Otherwise, the choice stays with them. I still find that hard sometimes, but it is more useful than treating my preference as another approval gate.

When a decision goes badly, I need to ask what the person knew at the time and whether they had the context and authority we said they had. There may be a judgement to improve, or a responsibility they did not meet. There may also be something I failed to provide. We need to be able to examine that together. If ownership lasts only until the first disappointing result, the safer choice next time is to bring every decision back to me.

The same standard applies when I get it wrong. For the front-end communication, repeating my intention would not repair the gap. I owed the team a usable record of the commitment. An apology matters, but they also need to see what I will do differently when the next decision arrives.

## The part that was mine

I still want to [get out of my team's way](/blog/category/thoughts/managing-smart-people). For that to work, I have to keep the context current, make decisions visible, answer questions, and accept sound choices I would not have made myself.

For the front-end commitment, my part is to leave a decision people can find and use. For the new engineer's goal, it is to make his authority clear and leave him room to exercise it. The agreement gives me a way to do both. I am working on the follow-through: bringing the missing context, answering the question, and explaining when something has changed enough to reopen a decision. Those are things I can do when someone needs me, without taking the work back from them.

## A few useful reads

- Gregor Ojstersek, [How to Build Trust as an Engineering Leader](https://newsletter.eng-leadership.com/p/how-to-build-trust-as-an-engineering). The public introduction makes the case for earning trust before changing how a team works; the rest is subscriber-only.
- Guillermo A. Fisher, [Trust and engineering excellence](https://leaddev.com/culture/trust-ultimate-driver-engineering-excellence). On clear expectations, admitting uncertainty and responding to feedback.
- Chris Hill, [The Power of Trust in Engineering Leadership](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/power-trust-engineering-leadership-chris-hill-ndqnc). On making room for people to challenge decisions, including when they are new to a team.
- Matt Whetton, [Trust, controls and engineering agents](https://medium.com/@mattwhetton/trust-is-the-most-effective-control-in-engineering-and-it-does-not-transfer-to-agents-54b017c4647d). An account of controls accumulating in an agent project, and a useful prompt to examine their lasting cost.
- Rouan Wilsenach, [Ship / Show / Ask](https://martinfowler.com/articles/ship-show-ask.html). Written for branching, and the vocabulary we use for when a change needs someone else before it lands. The useful move is the middle lane.
- Henrik Kniberg, [Spotify engineering culture, part 1](https://blog.crisp.se/2014/03/27/henrikkniberg/spotify-engineering-culture-part-1). The aligned-autonomy quadrant is the picture behind this post: high alignment is what makes high autonomy safe.
- Netflix, [Culture](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture). “Context, not control” is the shortest statement of why ownership has to run both ways.
