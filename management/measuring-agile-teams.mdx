## Why metrics are complicated

Let me tell you about metrics: they're complicated. I've been on teams that tracked nothing, and it was like flying blind. Are we going to make the release date? Are we getting faster or slower? No idea. I've also been on teams where metrics were used as weapons: pitting teams against each other, or justifying why everyone should work the weekend. Neither extreme is healthy.

Used thoughtfully, metrics do three things for a team: they reduce confusion, they show where you're actually making progress, and they surface where you're struggling early enough to act. The key word is *thoughtfully*, and most of this post is about what that looks like for each of the classic agile charts.

One framing before the charts: every metric below measures the **process**, not the **product**. Shipping on time isn't enough if you're shipping the wrong thing, so process metrics only mean something next to business metrics.

## Start from the business, not the board

Something that took me a while to learn: you need to ship the *right* features at the *right* time for the *right* market, and no burndown chart will tell you whether you did.

<Callout type='warning'>
**Important**: Your business metrics should tie directly to your roadmap and business goals, not just development activity.
</Callout>

For each major initiative, set a few key performance indicators that matter to the business. For each feature, define success criteria upfront: adoption rate, coverage, whatever fits. These create the feedback loop your process metrics plug into. Without them, a team can be a model of agile hygiene while confidently building things nobody wants.

## The classic charts, and how each one lies

Every chart below is genuinely useful, and every one of them can be gamed or misread. So for each: what it tells you, and how it lies.

### Sprint burndown

The sprint burndown tracks remaining work through the sprint: time on the x-axis, work left (story points or hours) on the y-axis, ideally reaching zero by the sprint's end. A team that consistently meets its forecast is the best internal advertisement for agile there is.

How it lies: the numbers are easy to fudge. Declaring an item done before it really is makes the chart look great this sprint and destroys the learning it exists to produce. Watch for these patterns:

- Finishing early sprint after sprint: the team is under-committing.
- Missing the forecast sprint after sprint: the team is over-committing.
- Steep cliffs instead of a gradual slope: work isn't broken down into small enough pieces.
- Scope appearing mid-sprint: the product owner is changing the deal after it was struck.

### Epic and release burndown

The same idea at a larger scale: progress across a body of work spanning many sprints. Here's the nuance that trips teams up: scope change at this level is *healthy*. As the team learns, the product owner should be adding and removing work. "Scope creep" is a problem inside a sprint; across an epic, it's the whole point of being agile. The chart's job is to keep that ebb and flow visible to everyone.

How it lies: mostly by neglect. Forecasts that never get updated as the team churns through work, several iterations with no visible progress, scope growing faster than the team absorbs it, or an epic that never ships anything incrementally along the way. Chronic, unexamined scope growth usually means the product owner doesn't yet understand the problem the epic is meant to solve.

### Velocity

Velocity is the team's average completed work per sprint, and it's the forecasting tool: if the backlog holds 500 points and the team averages 50 a sprint, you can reasonably plan around ten sprints. The more iterations behind the average, the better the forecast. New teams should expect velocity to climb as they gel; mature teams should expect it to hold steady, and a sustained drop is a retro topic, not a stick.

How it lies: velocity is only comparable to itself. Each team's estimation culture is unique, so team A at 50 points versus team B at 75 tells you *nothing* about who delivers more. The moment velocity is compared across teams or turned into a target, people re-inflate estimates and the number stops meaning anything. If velocity swings erratically, revisit estimation in the retro: were there challenges you couldn't see when estimating? Is outside pressure pushing commitments past reality? Are you simply forecasting with too much optimism?

### Cycle time and the control chart

Cycle time is how long one item takes from "in progress" to "done", and the control chart plots it per issue with a rolling average. It's the primary Kanban metric, but Scrum teams benefit just as much. What you want is a short, *consistent* cycle time regardless of work type, because consistency is what makes delivery predictable. It's also the fastest feedback loop in this list: change your process this week, and cycle time reacts almost immediately.

How it lies: through outliers and noise. Don't chase every spike; look for trends. Rising cycle time saps agility and deserves a retro (with one honest exception: if you expanded your definition of done, cycle time expanding with it is correct, not a regression). Erratic cycle time for similarly-sized items points at estimation misses: filter the chart by story-point value and look at each band separately.

### Cumulative flow

The cumulative flow diagram shows how many items sit in each workflow state over time, and healthy flow reads as smooth, roughly parallel bands. Bubbles or gaps in a band are shortages and bottlenecks made visible.

How it lies: quietly, at the edges. A blocked band means backups in one state and starvation downstream, and items stuck in intermediate states like "In Review" aren't just idle: the longer they sit, the nastier the integration conflicts when they finally merge. The other classic: a backlog band growing forever because obsolete items never get closed. That's not demand, that's hoarding.

## Beyond the process charts

Two more families of metrics deserve a place, and they're the ones that keep process metrics honest:

**Quality**: how many defects are found during development versus after release versus by people outside the team? How many get deferred to a future release? What's the customer-support request volume doing? What percentage of testing is automated? A great velocity with a rotten defect trend is a team borrowing from its future.

**Delivery**: how often do releases actually go out, and how many builds make it to production? How long does an emergency fix take to ship? Is a release routine, or does it require heroics? That last question is my favorite smell test: heroic releases are a process bug wearing a cape.

## Finding the balance

Metrics give you quantitative insight and measurable goals, and that's all they give you. The qualitative half, what the team says in retrospectives, matters just as much for trust, quality, and speed. Use both. And when a number and the team disagree about how things are going, the number is usually the one missing context.
