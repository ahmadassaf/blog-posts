I was part of a startup as it grew from six people to 400 and became a unicorn. I am now building another from scratch at Mav9, as CTO. This handbook is where I want to put what I learned from both, what I think we should do, and how we put those decisions into practice.

Each chapter follows one part of building and running a company, seen through a CTO's eyes. The company's stage matters too. A practice that helped at 400 people may be more than a team of ten needs. Most of the operating examples describe our work at Mav9. I use the earlier startup's experience where it changes how I approach them.

[Becoming a CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-nobody-handed-me-a-manual) started from the fact that nobody handed me a manual. Those posts were about what the job asked of me in the first year: the self-doubt, the trust I had left people to infer, the planning we redid four times, the controls I wrote before we had them.

Each of those posts is a story. The rules we agreed, the templates and the cadences are scattered through them, usually somewhere in the middle. This handbook collects them by subject, alongside what I have learned and what I would do differently.

## These are living posts

These chapters are pages I expect to keep editing as we change how we work, as something stops working, or as I find out I was wrong.

That changes a few things about how to read them:

- The date at the top of a chapter is its latest revision. The changelog records substantial additions and changes in approach.
- The unfinished parts are listed at the end of each chapter. I would rather show the gap than fill it with advice I have not tested.
- I revise sections in place, so an older link still points at the current version. The [Updates page](/blog/handbook/updates) records new chapters, substantial new sections and changes in methodology.
- New chapters will appear in the index below as I add them.

If you are reading this a year after the date above and a chapter has not moved, treat it with the suspicion you would give any wiki page nobody has touched.

## How each chapter is laid out

I try to distinguish experience from recommendation. Where I describe something we did, I give the context. Where I say what I think we should do, it may still be a proposal. The CTO's part runs through each subject: the decisions I own, where I need to work with others, and what I have to put in place.

The sections headed **How we do it at Mav9** describe the rule, cadence, template or tool, with proposals and unfinished work identified. I used that layout for [the engineering foundations](/blog/category/management/setting-the-engineering-foundations). Writing down what we actually do makes the gaps visible, especially when the answer is “not yet”.

The [Playbooks](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks) chapter adapts our documented management procedures alongside my experience with performance management. The [Appendices](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-appendices) contain versions of the plans and meeting notes that someone can copy. A documented procedure and a process we have used successfully are different claims; I say which one I can make.

The Mav9 parts are there as a template. We are about ten engineers building software for finance, with founders in two countries and no product manager. Some of what works for us will not fit a different team. I would copy the questions before the answers.

I leave teammates' names out, as I did in the series.

## Where the structure comes from

The shape is borrowed from [The Great CEO Within](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL20791757W) by Matt Mochary, with Alex MacCaw and Misha Talavera, which goes from the individual, to the group, to the tools and processes a company runs on. I have kept that order: my own habits first, then how we decide things together, then what we had to build so that it did not all depend on who was in the room.

It is a CEO's book, so I have changed what goes in each part to reflect my experience. The current chapters cover planning, review, release and compliance, and the management playbooks from our own handbook have a chapter of their own. I will add other parts of building a company as I have something useful to write about them.

## Index

1. [The Beginning](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-the-beginning). Taking the role, choosing the first team, setting a direction, and what a founding CTO ends up doing that is not engineering.
2. [Individual Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-individual-habits). How I keep track of my own work: the lists I keep, writing things down the second time, asking for help, and working with an assistant.
3. [Group Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits). How we decide things and keep our word: decisions that look decided, who has the final say, the two sides of ownership, transparency, and our values.
4. [Infrastructure](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-infrastructure). The things a team runs on apart from code: where knowledge lives, how goals are tracked, who is responsible for what, removing single points of failure, and the few numbers we look at.
5. [Collaboration](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-collaboration). Meetings and their cadence, feedback, and how a team of ten is organised without a product manager.
6. [Processes](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-processes). The routines for each recurring piece of work: planning, shipping a change, releasing, hiring and compliance.
7. [Playbooks](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks). Step-by-step guides for managing people: a new starter's first ninety days, one-to-ones, goal setting, feedback, performance reviews, low performance, recognition and succession.
8. [Appendices](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-appendices). Templates and checklists pulled out of the chapters, and the reading I keep going back to.

## What is missing

Incidents and post-mortems, support and the finance and legal work that landed on me still need fuller accounts. I have listed the gaps at the end of each chapter so they are easy to find, and I will fill them in as I work out what I think.

If something here is wrong, or you do it differently and it works, I would like to hear about it. That is most of the reason for writing it [in public](/blog/category/thoughts/learning-in-public).
