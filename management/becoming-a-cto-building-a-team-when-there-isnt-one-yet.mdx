We're about ten engineers and no product manager. A few months in, the engineers asked for one. Scope was unclear, priorities moved, and it was hard to know what “done” meant on any given ticket. All of that was true.

We wanted to hire a PM, but we couldn't yet afford to. That left me with a question I found harder than the hiring conversation: what were we going to do about the work that person would have owned in the meantime?

I had already spent quite a lot of time on a different answer. I had built a dashboard.

## The window only I had open

The real work lived in pull requests. Our project board was not reliably linked to them, so it was always a little wrong. Because it was wrong, nobody looked at it. Because nobody looked at it, nobody updated it, which made it more wrong.

My dashboard sat on top of that board. It had views for delivery, risk, gates, and people. I liked it. Nobody but me opened it. I was running engineering from a window only I had open, fed by a board only I maintained, and then wondering why the picture was unclear to everyone else.

When I pulled the numbers for the month, most merged pull requests did not close an issue. A large share of the ones that did were mine. That told me something about how we recorded work. It did not tell me who was contributing most, because most of the work was missing from that particular view.

The dashboard had given me somewhere to arrange the information I wanted. It had not given the team a reason to supply it. Keeping the board current was an extra task, and the benefit mostly came back to me.

Even a perfectly current board would not tell an engineer why a priority had changed, which customer problem mattered most, or who could settle an argument about scope. I was treating visibility as though it would provide direction.

## The work we still owed the team

A product manager could help us understand customer needs, choose what to pursue, define useful outcomes, and decide what to leave out. Those responsibilities did not disappear because we couldn't hire someone. They remained with leadership, including me.

We also had engineering habits to improve. Linking a pull request to its issue, testing beyond the edge of a ticket, and flagging a change in scope belonged to the people doing the work. A PM could help make those habits easier. Hiring one would not remove our responsibility for them.

AI was already helping us explore approaches and build changes faster. I can use it to draft a ticket or pull updates together too. The team still needed me to explain whose problem we were solving, why it mattered now, and what we were giving up to do it.

Those answers sometimes require information we haven't got yet. I need to make time to understand the customer's problem and involve engineers in deciding what to do about it.

I started writing down those responsibilities. It helped me separate the hiring question from the work we already owed the team. I could set out what leadership needed to provide and what I was asking engineers to take responsibility for, with both open to challenge. It was a proposal for us to discuss; putting it on paper did not mean we had agreed it or changed the habits.

The draft put obligations on leadership as well as engineers. Every goal needed to say who it was for, the problem it addressed, and what done would look like. Priorities needed one shared order. If we changed that order, we had to agree what moved out, changed or waited. Adding a request without making that tradeoff would leave the team to absorb it or make the decision themselves.

We also owed the team access to the reasoning: notes from customer conversations, opportunities to join those calls, and decisions recorded where the work was happening. A decision made on a call needed to reach the shared record that day. Otherwise, the person doing the work would still depend on finding someone who remembered the conversation.

The proposed response time was one working day for a scope question, concern or draft. In return, an engineer would bring what they understood, what was missing, and a recommendation. I would need to make time to answer. Asking people to raise uncertainty early made little sense if I then left their questions unanswered.

If someone tells me the scope is wrong and I respond as though they are being difficult, the invitation to challenge it means very little.

## Ownership starts before the code and continues after it

The draft asked every engineer to be able to use and explain the application's main customer journeys. For the part they owned, the understanding needed to go deeper: the user, the problem, the expected behaviour, the tradeoffs, and the evidence that the change worked. That gives an engineer a basis for questioning a requirement or spotting a problem that crosses the edge of their ticket.

Each piece of work still needed one clear owner. Understanding the wider product would help that person recognise dependencies and know whom to involve. It would not give them authority over every part of it. In a separate, later agreement on how we work, we wrote down the [Owner and Sponsor roles](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design): the Owner takes responsibility for delivery, while the Sponsor holds the product context and approves changes to the agreed scope.

The definition of finished work mattered just as much. The draft required the owner to take a change through to preproduction and personally use the affected journey end to end, including its failure cases. A merged pull request was one step in that process. Evidence should fit the change, and known limitations or follow-up work should be visible to the next person. Production release would still have its own approval requirements.

That expectation extends to handoffs. If deployment or verification remains, the shared record should say so and name who takes it next. Otherwise, one person's finished task becomes another person's unexplained gap. I wanted ownership to cover the useful result and the work needed to get there.

## Giving the board a job people could see

The draft tried to give those expectations a place in meetings we already had: coordinate from the shared record, name area owners in planning, and use demos to walk through the whole application. I wanted to make better use of the time we already spent together. We have since [changed the meeting cadence](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year).

The tools also needed distinct jobs. GitHub held delivery work and its status; Notion held the durable product context and decisions; the dashboard brought them together. The proposal asked people to update the source rather than repeat the same update in three places. If the dashboard was misleading, we needed a concrete example to fix. If a step added overhead without helping anyone, we needed to simplify it.

The board's job in that proposal was to help someone understand the state of work and get a decision. For a blocked item, the draft asked for what was missing, who could help, and the next action. That was a more useful request than asking everyone to keep statuses tidy. It connected the update I wanted to the help the engineer needed.

I kept the per-person numbers out of the team document. [Ranking people by an incomplete dataset](/blog/category/management/measuring-agile-teams) would have encouraged a conversation about who looked busy. I wanted us to discuss the missing connection between decisions, issues, and code.

The draft gave me a concrete conversation to have with the team. We could take a piece of work and ask whether its purpose was clear, who could decide a scope change, and what evidence would show it was finished. The later Owner and Sponsor agreement supplied the decision roles. The broader habits in the draft were still something to work through together.

## Whether we need a dedicated PM

Maybe we won't need a dedicated PM. If the founders can stay close to customers, make the priority calls and give engineers enough context to own delivery, that may be enough for our team. AI can help with drafting and keeping information moving. I want to see whether we can do the product work well this way, without it all quietly landing back on me.

More customer requests than we could triage in a week would be one reason to revisit the hire. I would also worry if we kept choosing from the existing backlog because nobody had time to find out what was missing. Those are gaps I could hire someone to address. Having ten engineers on its own doesn't tell me whether we need that person.

If we hire, I want someone who has time to follow a customer problem properly and can tell us when the work already on our roadmap is the wrong bet. I would want to know what they had learned that changed our priorities. The engineers need to be in those conversations too, helping question the proposed solution and working out what it would take.

Hiring that person would still leave engineers responsible for understanding users, clarifying requirements, checking the experience and communicating progress. That is the team I want a PM to join.

For now, I am starting with the decisions already on my desk: set the priority, explain the customer problem, and answer when the scope no longer fits. The draft puts those obligations beside what I ask of the engineers. It gives them a way to question my part of the work, just as the board gives me a way to ask about theirs.

## A few useful reads

- Ryan Singer's [Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup). The chapters on appetite and boundaries are the ones I reread. They are useful when I need to turn a broad request into work a team can make decisions about.
- Sarah Milstein's [How team agreements promote high performance](https://leaddev.com/culture/how-team-agreements-promote-high-performance). A practical companion to writing an agreement, including a distinction relevant to our situation: good working habits do not make unclear priorities disappear.
- Marty Cagan's [Product is Hard](https://vimeo.com/333559833). I would pair this with the agreement article when discussing the PM role. It keeps the conversation connected to product value, beyond how neatly the engineering work is tracked.
- Vin Vashishta's [Do we still need Product Managers and Product Owners?](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/vineetvashishta_do-we-still-need-product-managers-and-product-share-7462492123435589632-1YxR/). On bringing new information and opportunities into a business as AI speeds up delivery.
- David Pereira's [Are PMs Getting to the End of the Road?](https://davidavpereira.medium.com/are-pms-getting-to-the-end-of-the-road-f2d196eb9155). On the customer understanding and difficult decisions behind useful product work.
