We're about ten engineers and no product manager. A few months in, the engineers asked for one. Scope was unclear, priorities moved, and it was hard to know what “done” meant on any given ticket. All of that was true.

We wanted to hire a PM, but we couldn't yet afford to. That left me with a question I found harder than the hiring conversation: what were we going to do about the work that person would have owned in the meantime?

I had already spent quite a lot of time on a different answer. I had built a dashboard.

## The window only I had open

The real work lived in pull requests. Our project board was not reliably linked to them, so it was always a little wrong. Because it was wrong, nobody looked at it. Because nobody looked at it, nobody updated it, which made it more wrong.

My dashboard sat on top of that board. It had views for delivery, risk, gates, and people. I liked it. Nobody but me opened it. I was running engineering from a window only I had open, fed by a board only I maintained, and then wondering why the picture was unclear to everyone else.

When I pulled the numbers for the month, most merged pull requests did not close an issue. A large share of the ones that did were mine. That told me something about how we recorded work. It did not tell me who was contributing most, because most of the work was missing from that particular view.

Even a perfectly current board would not tell an engineer why a priority had changed, which customer problem mattered most, or who could settle an argument about scope. I was treating visibility as though it would provide direction.

## The work we still owed the team

A product manager could help us understand customer needs, choose what to pursue, define useful outcomes, and decide what to leave out. Those responsibilities did not disappear because we couldn't hire someone. They remained with leadership, including me.

AI was already helping us explore approaches and build changes faster. I can use it to draft a ticket or pull updates together too. The team still needed me to explain whose problem we were solving, why it mattered now, and what we were giving up to do it. Sometimes that meant finding out more about the customer's problem before asking an engineer to solve it.

I drafted a division of responsibilities for us to discuss. Leadership would explain who each goal was for, what problem it addressed and what finished work would look like. Engineers would question the scope, connect the code to the work and check the result. It was still a proposal, but it put my obligations beside what I was asking of everyone else.

Priorities needed one shared order. If we changed it, we had to agree what moved out, changed or waited. The proposal also asked us to share customer notes, involve engineers in those conversations, and record decisions where the work was happening. Otherwise, the person doing the work would depend on finding someone who remembered the call.

The proposed response time was one working day for a scope question, concern or draft. In return, an engineer would bring what they understood, what was missing, and a recommendation. I would need to make time to answer. Asking people to raise uncertainty early made little sense if I then left their questions unanswered.

## Ownership starts before the code and continues after it

The draft asked every engineer to be able to use and explain the application's main customer journeys. For the part they owned, they needed to understand the user, the expected behaviour and the tradeoffs well enough to question a requirement or spot a problem beyond the edge of their ticket.

That included checking the result. The draft asked the owner to take a change through to preproduction and personally use the affected journey end to end, including its failure cases. A merged pull request was one step in that process. Any remaining verification or deployment work needed a named owner; production release would still have its own approval requirements.

In a separate, later agreement, we wrote down the [Owner and Sponsor roles](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design). Those made clear who owned delivery and who could agree a change in scope.

## Giving the board a job people could see

GitHub held delivery work and its status; Notion held the durable product context and decisions; the dashboard brought them together. The proposal asked people to update the source, then use that shared record in meetings we already had.

For a blocked item, the draft asked for what was missing, who could help, and the next action.

I kept the per-person numbers out of the team document. [Ranking people by an incomplete dataset](/blog/category/management/measuring-agile-teams) would have encouraged a conversation about who looked busy. I wanted us to discuss the missing connection between decisions, issues, and code.

Later, the dashboard became useful to the team when I gave it a view of goal status for [stand-up](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year). It had a place in a conversation we were already having. It could help us see the work and the decisions it needed. Choosing the priorities still belonged to us.

## Whether we need a dedicated PM

Maybe we won't need a dedicated PM. If the founders can stay close to customers, make the priority calls and give engineers enough context to own delivery, that may be enough for our team. AI can help with drafting and keeping information moving. I want to see whether we can do the product work well this way, without it all quietly landing back on me.

More customer requests than we could triage in a week would be one reason to revisit the hire. I would also worry if we kept choosing from the existing backlog because nobody had time to find out what was missing. Those are gaps I could hire someone to address. Having ten engineers on its own doesn't tell me whether we need that person.

If we hire, I want someone who has time to follow a customer problem properly and can tell us when the work already on our roadmap is the wrong bet. The engineers need to be in those conversations too, questioning the proposed solution and working out what it would take. Hiring a PM would still leave them responsible for understanding and checking the experience they build.

For now, I am starting with the decisions already on my desk: set the priority, explain the customer problem, and answer when the scope no longer fits.

## A few useful reads

- Ryan Singer's [Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup). The chapters on appetite and boundaries are the ones I reread. They are useful when I need to turn a broad request into work a team can make decisions about.
- Sarah Milstein's [How team agreements promote high performance](https://leaddev.com/culture/how-team-agreements-promote-high-performance). A practical companion to writing an agreement, including a distinction relevant to our situation: good working habits do not make unclear priorities disappear.
- Marty Cagan's [Product is Hard](https://vimeo.com/333559833). I would pair this with the agreement article when discussing the PM role. It keeps the conversation connected to product value, beyond how neatly the engineering work is tracked.
- Vin Vashishta's [Do we still need Product Managers and Product Owners?](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/vineetvashishta_do-we-still-need-product-managers-and-product-share-7462492123435589632-1YxR/). On bringing new information and opportunities into a business as AI speeds up delivery.
- David Pereira's [Are PMs Getting to the End of the Road?](https://davidavpereira.medium.com/are-pms-getting-to-the-end-of-the-road-f2d196eb9155). On the customer understanding and difficult decisions behind useful product work.
