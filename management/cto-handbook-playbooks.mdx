*This is a living chapter of [The Startup Handbook](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook). I edit it as our practice changes; the changelog is at the bottom.*

The other chapters say what I believe and what we do. A playbook is narrower: the steps for one situation, written so that a manager facing it for the first time has somewhere to start. These come from the management playbook in the Mav9 handbook, condensed. Where a playbook and our day-to-day practice differ, I will note it here as I revise each one.

## Starting as a manager

The playbook opens with advice for someone leading a team for the first time.

- **Set the tone in the first team meeting.** Share your expectations about feedback, meetings and logistics. Say what you do not know yet and ask for feedback on how it is going.
- **Look for quick wins.** Ask the team what they should stop doing and what the worst part of their job is. Removing one of those builds rapport quickly.
- **Your success is the team's output.** It is tempting to jump into every fire yourself, especially if that used to be your job.
- **Set goals and write them down,** so the next meeting has something to check progress against.
- **Shield the team when a request is unreasonable,** and look for a middle ground when it is not.
- **Do not attend every meeting you are invited to.** If you add nothing, say so.
- **Respect the maker's schedule.** People producing work need long blocks; coordination runs on a different clock.
- **Hold regular one-to-ones, find a mentor, and keep learning.**
- **Say thank you, specifically.**
- **Think of yourself as a coach.**

I wrote about the third point in [My First Year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much).

## The first ninety days

**Purpose.** Give a new starter the clarity, connections and feedback to make an impact, and find and correct any problems early.

**Before they start.** The manager writes a ninety-day plan with clear, measurable goals, at least seven days before the start date.

**First week.** Share the plan and set expectations. It covers their goals for the ninety days, who they should get to know, and what to read or use.

**Throughout.**

- One-to-ones at least weekly.
- A check-in at the end of the first and second month against the goals in the plan.
- Notes of what was discussed kept in the plan, and shared with the new starter so they can see everything that is recorded.
- The new starter is expected to ask questions, give their own feedback, and say what extra support they need.

**At ninety days.** A review meeting with one of three outcomes:

1. They pass, and the manager sets longer-term goals.
2. They made clear progress and fell just short. The period can be extended, usually by one month and by no more than three, and only once.
3. They missed the expectations of the role, and employment is likely to end.

If there are concerns, the manager raises them as soon as they appear, well before the review.

## One-to-ones

**Cadence.** At least every two weeks, usually thirty minutes. Weekly during onboarding or a demanding project. Both people are responsible for making sure they happen.

**Who owns the agenda.** The report does. The manager may add topics, such as progress on goals. Larger subjects, such as development and career, come up at least once a month.

**Template.** Four questions for the report to prepare:

- What am I proud of accomplishing?
- What am I focused on next?
- Where do I need feedback?
- What are my next steps for development?

Close by confirming who is responsible for what, the next steps, any new deadline and when you will discuss it again.

**Keeping track.** One shared document that both people add to, with notes of decisions, dates and follow-ups.

**For the manager.**

- Keep a consistent cadence and do not cancel.
- Make sure the setting is private.
- Ask what would make the meeting most useful to them.
- Start each one knowing what you want from it.
- Balance positive and constructive feedback.
- Coach before you solve: help the person find the answer.

## Setting goals with a report

Set goals in a new starter's first two weeks, then each quarter after a review.

- Agree three to five goals that support both the person's development and the team's objectives.
- Define the expectation in detail, and say what criteria you will use to evaluate it next time.
- Make each goal specific and measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. It should be clear afterwards whether it was met, ideally with evidence. “Run 5km in under 25 minutes by the end of June” passes; “get better at running” does not.

The playbook adds four more tests:

- **Committed.** Both of you agree with the goal. A slightly worse goal you both believe in beats a better one only the manager wants.
- **Owned.** The report writes it. If they do not own their development, nobody will.
- **Inspiring.** Not every goal has to be, but if none are, something is wrong.
- **Followed up.** Motivation is highest just after goals are set. Check regularly, and rewrite a goal that is no longer right.

These are personal goals for a report. Delivery goals, with an Owner and a Sponsor, are in [Infrastructure](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-infrastructure).

## Giving feedback

Tie feedback to a concrete situation. Be specific, and avoid “never” and “always”.

**Stop, start, keep.**

- Keep: “You excel at [action], and I would love to keep seeing that from you.”
- Keep, with a third party: “I encourage you to keep doing [action]. I have had feedback that it helped the team [result].”
- Stop: “My feedback is for you to stop [action] because it results in [consequence].” Use it for something observed more than once.
- Start: “I encourage you to start [action] because it will help you [result].” Often easier to hear than a stop.

**Situation, behaviour, impact.** Name the situation, describe what the person did, and say what it led to. For constructive feedback, add what they could have done differently.

**How as well as what.** Recognise how someone worked as well as what they delivered: achievement, communication, craft, growth, leadership and teamwork, tied to our values.

How I try to receive feedback is in [Collaboration](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-collaboration).

## The performance review meeting

Nothing in a review should be a surprise. Anything raised there should already have come up in a one-to-one.

**Preparing.**

- Give as much notice as possible and meet in person where you can.
- Assess performance against the person's goals before reading their self-evaluation, so their reflections do not sway yours. Then compare the two.
- Plan the points to cover and check yourself for bias across the whole team.

**In the meeting.**

1. The report talks through their self-assessment first.
2. The manager follows with theirs, mostly emphasising and adding what was not covered.
3. Agree goals for the next period and concrete next steps.
4. Thank them.

Aim for the report to do about seventy per cent of the talking. Ask open questions and listen. Use clear, change-focused language, and ask what support they need.

**Questions that help.**

- What are you most proud of since the last review?
- What do you enjoy most about your work?
- What do people on the team rely on you for?
- What would you like to focus on next?
- What is holding you back?
- How can I improve as a manager?

## Rating performance and potential

A rating covers how someone works as well as what they deliver.

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Not meeting expectations. The manager should already be discussing this and supporting the person to improve. |
| 2 | Partially meeting expectations, or new to the role and not yet fully ramped up. |
| 3 | Meeting all expectations. Most people should be here. |
| 4 | Exceeding expectations consistently, and possibly ready for a broader role. |
| 5 | Outstanding. Visible across the company, influencing others, ready for more responsibility. |

Potential is rated separately on three points, from ability, aspiration and engagement: high, medium, and matched to the current role. A “matched” rating is not a criticism. Someone may be happy where they are, and potential moves with what else is going on in their life.

## Calibration

Managers meet to compare ratings so that the same standard applies to everyone at the same level.

**Before the meeting.**

- For each person: what they delivered, how they worked, how they did against goals, and how they grew.
- Across your team: how people at the same level compare, and what separates those above and below expectations.
- Bring concrete examples.

**In the meeting.** Listen to how other managers apply the standard and be prepared to change your rating.

**Checking for bias.**

- What is my emotional response to this person?
- Am I only thinking about recent examples?
- Do I have evidence from more than one source?
- Does this person or situation remind me of someone else?

## Managing low performance

Deal with minor concerns informally and early, with coaching and more feedback. Do not delay when it continues.

**Stage one: a documented conversation.**

1. Focus on the opportunity to improve.
2. Set out the concern with examples and the standard that is not being met.
3. Let the person respond. Pause the meeting if they need time.
4. Agree actions, support, training and deadlines.
5. Be honest about what happens if nothing changes.
6. Send a written summary within twenty-four hours.

**Stage two: a formal improvement plan.** Used when stage one has not worked. It sets specific, observable expectations over a fixed period, with weekly reviews. The aim is for the person to succeed. The possible outcomes are that they meet the plan, that it is extended once after real progress, or that they leave.

Throughout, the conversation is two-way and private. If a disability or other underlying reason may be involved, raise it at the start so adjustments can be considered.

## Recognition

- Make it frequent, or it looks contrived.
- Be specific about what you appreciated, and cite the example.
- Pass on praise you hear from others, quickly.
- Know who prefers public recognition and who prefers private.
- Offer more responsibility, a seat in a meeting they would not normally attend, or a learning opportunity.
- Act on a suggestion. It shows what you think of their judgement.
- Perks matter far less than trust in a manager.

At Mav9 the weekly All hands has a slot for this: Owners demo shipped goals and recognise the people who helped.

## Psychological safety

Amy Edmondson defines it as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. She gives three things a leader can do:

- Frame the work as a learning problem.
- Acknowledge your own fallibility.
- Model curiosity and ask a lot of questions.

Practices from the playbook:

- Gather opinions on an important decision in writing before meeting to discuss it.
- Let everyone put ideas forward before you say which you support.
- Thank people who challenge your view.
- Make sure people with less authority on paper are heard.

Edmondson's questions for measuring it ask how strongly people agree with statements such as: mistakes are held against you on this team; members can bring up problems and tough issues; it is safe to take a risk; it is difficult to ask other members for help.

I wrote about doing the second of Edmondson's three. I told the team where I had fallen short and asked them to explain their parts of the system to me; see [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

## Succession planning

The aim is that a key departure or promotion does not stop the company.

1. Identify critical and emerging positions.
2. Identify what makes someone successful in each.
3. Assess who could step up or move across.
4. Develop those people.
5. Measure and review.

Open roles are offered internally before we look outside, and the critical knowledge for each position is written down so it can be passed on. That last part overlaps with the section on single points of failure in [Infrastructure](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-infrastructure).
