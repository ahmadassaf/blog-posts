## What is a kata?

Here's a concept that changed how I think about solving problems. If you've ever studied martial arts, you know what a kata is: a pattern of movements you practice over and over until it becomes second nature. Improvement Kata applies that idea to how we tackle challenges at work.

The concept comes from Toyota (yes, them again; between this and [Kanban](/blog/management/introduction-to-classical-agile), they really did figure some things out). Mike Rother documented it in [Toyota Kata](https://www.amazon.com/Toyota-Kata-Managing-Improvement-Adaptiveness/dp/0071635238), and the core idea is developing a *habit* of scientific thinking. Instead of winging it every time you face a problem, you follow one consistent pattern. Repeat it enough and it becomes automatic: you stop thinking about the pattern and start thinking only about the problem.

That's the difference between this and the "continuous improvement" every team claims to practice. A retro action item is an intention. A kata is a rehearsed behavior.

## The four steps

The pattern breaks big, fuzzy goals into small targets you can actually hit:

1. **Understand the direction.** Where are we trying to go? What's the big goal? If you struggle to name it, look at how your team contributes to the organization's larger goals, or where your team's strengths could turn a vision into reality. Call it a North Star: everything else in the kata steers by it.
2. **Grasp the current condition.** Where are we, honestly? Document the current process, check the team against the metrics that matter, and write down what you actually know versus what you assume. This step only works if you're brutally honest; a flattering baseline poisons every step after it.
3. **Define the next target condition.** Not the final destination, just the next milestone: a single, tangible change achievable in a few weeks or sprints that moves you toward the direction. Small enough to hit, big enough to matter.
4. **Iterate toward it through experiments.** Form a hypothesis about how to get there, try it, and treat every failure as course-correction data rather than a penalty. Obstacles aren't exceptions to the plan; discovering and clearing them *is* the plan.

Two working habits make step four effective. First, move fast even if it means cutting corners: you either get early confirmation the approach works, or you surface the unforeseen problems while they're still cheap. Second, once it works, go back and polish the corners you cut. Speed first, then cleanliness, in that order and never the first without the second.

## Why it works

The value isn't mystical; it comes from three mundane effects compounding:

- **Alignment**: when everyone can name the direction and the current target, people make proactive decisions toward the goal instead of waiting for assignments. Shared definitions of success are what turn a group into a team.
- **Experimentation over paralysis**: knowing the destination without knowing the path usually freezes people. A rehearsed experiment loop dissolves that: you don't need the whole route, only a testable next step.
- **Less waste**: the constant question "does this move me toward the current target?" is a ruthless filter. It catches the features nobody needs and the rabbit holes that consume a week before anyone asks why. Managers practicing the same kata spend their time clearing the team's actual obstacles instead of inventing new work.

## What it looks like in practice

Say you want to build a new service around an idea you're not sure will work. The kata version: instead of architecting every layer upfront and expanding to feature-complete, pick a target that delivers some real value and moves toward the system you envision. Run at it, learn from what breaks, adjust, and when you hit the target, reassess and pick the next one. Each cycle either confirms the direction or redirects you before the sunk cost grows.

Or take an internal system that needs improvements. Pick one improvement you're ready to tackle, brainstorm approaches, and get the fastest version working to start collecting feedback. Good enough? Move to the next problem. The discipline is in finishing each cycle before starting the next.

## Kata and lean

People mix these up because both came out of Toyota, but they sit at different levels. **Lean** is about processes: extracting the most value from a workflow with minimal waste, then optimizing that workflow relentlessly. **Kata** is about people: an individual-level habit of learning through experimentation, most useful exactly where lean struggles, in problems full of uncertainty where no process exists yet to optimize.

They compose beautifully. Lean names the goal (eliminate waste, maximize value); kata provides the daily mechanism (experiment, learn, discard what doesn't work). Kata's insistence on finishing one target before starting the next feeds lean's efficiency directly, and breaking objectives into small optimized pieces is how a system ends up lean as a whole. If lean describes the future state you want, Improvement Kata is a tool you can use in the present state to get there.
