We are about ten engineers, with no compliance function, no product manager, and a CTO who also runs payroll. The way a company this size does the work of a larger one is with tools, and for the last year the most useful tool has been an AI assistant that can read our systems, draft what we need, and run the same procedure the same way every time.

This post is about where that has worked, and about the one line I keep having to redraw: the difference between an output that is useful and a decision that someone has to own.

## Evidence that already exists

Most of the evidence an auditor or a customer asks for already exists somewhere. Who approved a change is in the pull request. When it reached production is in the deployment log. Who has access to what is in the identity provider. The work is in collecting it, linking it, and presenting it so that a person who was not there can follow it.

That is a good job for an assistant. Ours reads from the systems of record, pulls the records for a given change or period, and drafts the document with a link beside every claim. I read the draft, open a sample of the links, and decide whether it shows what the request asked for.

Two rules sit at the top of every procedure that touches evidence: never fabricate a record, and never backdate one. If a record does not exist, the output says so. A gap in the evidence is information. A document that papers over it is a liability with a nice cover page.

## Procedures instead of memory

A surprising amount of running a small company is remembering to do things monthly. Review the DNS records. Log the capacity requests we raised with a cloud provider. Record which policies changed and why. Each one is small. Each one is easy to skip in a busy week, and each one is exactly what someone checking our work will ask about.

I have been writing these down as short procedures the assistant can run: what to look at, what to compare it with, what to record, and what to flag for a person. Writing the procedure turned out to be the valuable part. It forced me to say what the task actually involves, which I had been carrying in my head. The assistant running it is what makes the task happen on the second and tenth month rather than only the first.

The procedures are plain text in a repository. Anyone on the team can read what the assistant does, change it, or run it themselves. That matters more to me than the time saved. A chore I did from memory was mine. A written procedure is the company's.

## Reading policies against reality

A policy is a set of sentences about how the company works. Most of those sentences describe something that can be checked: a setting, a schedule, a record. In the [compliance post](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-compliance-is-a-mirror) I write about what happened when someone checked them for me. Before that, I had started asking the assistant to do a first pass.

It is good at this. Give it a procedure and access to the configuration the procedure describes, and it will list the sentences it cannot find evidence for. It is also good at reading two policies side by side and pointing out where they disagree with each other, which happens more often than I would like to admit after a year of edits.

What it cannot do is decide which side to change. When a policy says one thing and the system does another, somebody has to choose between implementing the sentence and rewriting it, and that choice depends on the risk the sentence was there to address. The assistant shortens the search. The decision stays with me.

## Cheaper to build, not cheaper to decide

The clearest change is in how much a senior engineer can build in a week. I wrote in the [prologue](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn) about hiring experienced people and expecting AI to multiply what they can do. A year in, that has held. Exploring an approach, producing something we can try, and working through the changes afterwards all take less time than they did.

The thing it did not change is the cost of a bad decision about what to build. The dashboard nobody opened, from the [team post](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet), took days rather than weeks. That made it easier to build without asking whether anyone needed it. When building gets cheap, deciding what to build is where the engineering judgement goes, and I want the senior people spending their attention there.

The test I use before accepting generated code is the one I would use for any pull request: can the person submitting it explain what it does and why, and would they merge it with their name on it? If the answer is no, the speed was not real.

## The review service I switched off

We built a small service that reviews pull requests with several models, each looking at a different concern: correctness, security, tests, the reach of a change, and whether we could reverse it. When they agreed, the service could approve a standard change.

The pilot caught things. It withdrew its approval when a reviewer opened a thread, and it held risky changes for a second engineer. I liked what it was doing, which made the next decision uncomfortable.

Then our auditor's guidance on AI arrived. For our change process, the output was to remain a recommendation rather than an approval. We took the service down that afternoon. The next day we put it back with the approval removed. It still reviews and raises findings. A human merges, and an independent human approves the step where a change reaches production. We kept the part that helped us inspect the work and changed the authority we had given it.

Putting a person at the end of the process does not settle it, though. Someone clicking through a reassuring model summary is not reviewing anything. Our answer so far is to present the findings for review, require the person merging to resolve each thread, and send large changes directly to a person. I still need the reviewer to understand the change and be willing to reject it. A resolved thread alone does not tell me that happened.

I've written about [making decisions](/blog/category/thoughts/making-decisions) under uncertainty. This was the first time I had reversed one in public within twenty-four hours, and the team was fine with it.

## Where the line is

For a team this size, the leverage is real and I want all of it. The assistant gathers, drafts, compares, runs the procedure, and finds the thing we missed. Without it, several of the things I describe in this series would simply not get done.

What I'm trying to catch is the moment a useful output starts standing in for work nobody has done. In evidence, that work is checking the records. In a policy review, it is choosing what to change. In code, it is understanding the change well enough to approve it. The tool can produce something that looks finished at every one of those points, and it is my name on the process when it is not.

## A few useful reads

- Simon Willison, [Not all AI-assisted programming is vibe coding (but vibe coding rocks)](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/). His test of whether you can explain the code is the one I use before accepting something an assistant has produced.
- Birgitta Böckeler, [The role of developer skills in agentic coding](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/13-role-of-developer-skills.html). Concrete examples of the steering and correction hidden inside a successful session with an agent. Useful when I'm trying to describe what our human review needs to involve.
- Thoughtworks, [Complacency with AI-generated code](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/complacency-with-ai-generated-code). A useful warning for the moment a tool has worked well enough that I start paying less attention to it.
