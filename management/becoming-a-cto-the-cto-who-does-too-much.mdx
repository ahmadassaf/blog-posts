When I pulled the numbers for our working agreement, one of them stopped me. Of the pull requests in a month that closed an issue, most were mine. My first reaction was that I was doing too much of the engineering.

That number could not tell me that. As I described in the [team post](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet), most pull requests did not link to an issue at all. I was looking at the part of the work we had recorded consistently, and mistaking it for the whole.

Still, the discomfort was worth following. I didn't need a ticket count to know that hiring, conversations with the auditor, and architecture decisions were getting the hours left over after I had finished something else. I knew what I kept choosing first.

## Work I can point to

I like finishing things. A certificate works, a migration runs, a pull request merges. There is a visible difference between the beginning and the end of the afternoon, and I can point to it.

A conversation is harder to account for that way. I can leave one believing we have made a better decision and have nothing to show for it until much later. Sometimes I will never know what would have happened without it. I find that uncertainty harder than a technical problem, even when the technical problem is unfamiliar.

There is another reason I reach for it. In the [imposter post](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real), I wrote about the voice that says a real CTO would have all of this under control. Finishing something gives me a reply. Here is a change I made. Here is a problem I solved. It feels like evidence that I belong in the job.

Delegating asks me to give up that reassurance and spend time explaining something I could perhaps finish faster myself. In the moment, doing it looks efficient. What that comparison leaves out is the next time the same kind of work arrives, and the person who could have learned to own it.

## The queue behind my calendar

We have about ten engineers. The awkward migration, the certificate, the policy, the dashboard cannot keep returning to one person simply because that person handled them last time.

I had helped create that pattern. I kept picking things up, and other people learned that I would. The dashboard nobody else opened was part of it: I held the picture, maintained it, and became the person who explained it. I had made myself useful in a way that was difficult to share.

Some of that work does belong with me. A small company needs people who can cross boundaries, and there will be times when my taking a technical task is the right choice. I don't have a sensible target number of tickets for a CTO. A week with no code could be a week spent avoiding a hard technical decision, just as a week full of code could be a week spent avoiding hiring.

The question I need to ask is what my involvement makes possible, and what waits while I'm involved. When the answer is that people need my availability to make ordinary progress, I have more to change than my calendar.

## Making room without disappearing

I'm writing down work I should hand over even though I can do it. I need that decision made before the task arrives, because the temptation is strongest when I can see exactly how to finish it. There will be exceptions. When I take something back during an urgent week, I want us to name that exception and agree when the ownership returns.

Code review is one place I'm changing this. I still review, but I'm trying to stop being the reviewer a change depends on. Other engineers need the context to make those judgements. The [AI review service](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature) can supply findings; the human responsibilities still need to be clear. Removing me from the queue is useful only if somebody else has enough context and authority to act.

The working agreement asks something similar of the board. It should carry the shared picture rather than require me to reconstruct it for each conversation. The agreement was mine to draft, but the meetings it describes need to work without me in the room. Handing over the document is a beginning. The people running those meetings need to understand what decisions they can make and why the meetings exist.

I also keep a log of work I did that someone else could have owned. It makes the habit visible to me. A single entry might be reasonable; seeing the same kind of entry again is a reason to ask why the handover has not happened.

I've written before about [defending the team's time](/blog/category/thoughts/managing-smart-people). I'm learning to apply that to my own time too. Hiring and longer-term decisions need room in the week while I still have the attention to give them. They cannot keep depending on how quickly I get through everything else.

## After the first year

At the start of this series, I described the job as being responsible when something breaks. I still feel that responsibility. I'm beginning to understand how much of it involves helping other people act without waiting for me.

That runs through the stories I have told here: asking before being certain, changing permissions, making product context available, and keeping a human answerable for work done with AI. The hard part has usually been changing something I was doing, even when I could explain perfectly well why the team needed to work differently.

I'm still working on it. I don't have a finished delegation system, or a calendar that proves I have become an executive. I have a clearer sense of the work I keep reaching for and the work that gets squeezed out. For now, that gives me something specific to change.

## A few useful reads

- Lara Hogan's [When to delegate, when to say no](https://larahogan.me/blog/when-to-delegate-when-to-say-no/). This is the exercise I used to sort my own work. It helps turn the vague feeling of having too much on my plate into decisions about individual commitments.
- Will Larson's [Work on what matters](https://staffeng.com/guides/work-on-what-matters/). The discussion of easy, satisfying work is particularly relevant to my habit of reaching for something I can finish before facing a harder decision.
- Charity Majors' [The Engineer/Manager Pendulum](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/hands-on-coding-managers/), the talk. The spoken version of the essay in the imposter post, and a useful counterweight to an absolute rule about managers and coding. I would read it alongside this post when deciding how to stay close to technical work without making the team depend on my availability.
