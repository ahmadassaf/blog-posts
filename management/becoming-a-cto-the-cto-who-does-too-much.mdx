During my first year as CTO, I hired engineers, set the initial architecture and built the front end. I also set up a UK company, ran payroll and found myself responsible for security policies. There was a product to build and a company around it that needed to work.

Those were real parts of the job. We had no front-end engineer at the start. The UK entity needed someone to get it running. A small founding team cannot wait until every responsibility has a specialist before doing the work.

Looking back, the harder part was recognising when the arrangement needed to change. Something I took on because nobody else could do it could remain mine long after that reason had gone. A decision I could explain in a conversation became harder to carry as more people built against it. The way we started was useful. It needed to evolve with the team and the product.

## Some work was mine until we could hand it over

The front end is the clearest example. Building it myself helped us get started. When we could hire for it, I handed it over. The company still needed that work, and now someone else could own it. That gave me room to spend more time on decisions affecting the rest of the product.

Other responsibilities have been harder to place. We still don't have a product manager, so [the product work remains with the founders](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet). We have to stay close to customers, choose priorities and explain what we want a piece of work to achieve. I started writing those obligations down because leaving the role unfilled could not mean leaving the work unowned.

Those two situations need different responses. For the front end, we could hire and transfer ownership. For product work, we needed to make the responsibilities within the existing team clearer. “Delegate more” would not have answered both.

I still expect to build things. Sometimes that is the most useful contribution I can make. I also keep a list of work I should hand over, and a log of work that came back to me. Looking at those together helps me notice when a temporary arrangement has quietly become permanent. Then I can ask what was missing from the handover: a person, access, context, authority or time to do it properly.

## The team needed a different kind of involvement

I had brought in people I trusted and set a technical direction. As they built, their understanding of individual parts became deeper than mine. Meanwhile, I held context from customer and founder conversations they hadn't been in. Both kinds of knowledge were necessary, and neither was reaching the other reliably.

Asking the engineers to explain how their parts of the system worked helped me regain the wider picture. It also helped with [the feeling that I should already know everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real). I could ask about a gap and do something with the answer.

I needed to make my side of that exchange more dependable too. The team needed to know which customer problem mattered, why a priority had changed and which decisions they could make themselves. Hiring capable people didn't make those answers obvious.

The [Sponsor role in our working agreement](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design) gives me a clearer part in that work: help shape the goal, supply the product context and decide scope changes with the Owner. They own delivery and the implementation decisions within that scope.

Writing that context down gives someone working at a different time or in a different place access to a decision without needing me on a call. It also gives them something specific to question when the decision stops making sense.

## What I want to carry into the next year

I have a clearer idea now of where my attention is needed. I need enough technical understanding to see when choices in different parts of the product conflict. I need to help the founders turn customer needs into priorities the team can act on. Hiring, difficult tradeoffs and questions that cross responsibilities need time of their own in my week.

I also need to keep making ownership practical. For review, the board and the meetings I want to hand over, naming someone is only a start. They need the information and authority to carry the responsibility, and a way to get help when a decision exceeds it. Those handovers are still in progress; the front end showed me what completing one can free up.

When I took the role, being responsible meant being the person who would pick something up when it needed doing. I still need to be willing to do that. Now I also need to decide how that work should be owned after the immediate problem is solved.

## A few useful reads

- Lara Hogan, [When to delegate, when to say no](https://larahogan.me/blog/when-to-delegate-when-to-say-no/). The exercise I used to sort my own commitments and work out which responsibilities needed to move.
- Will Larson, [Work on what matters](https://staffeng.com/guides/work-on-what-matters/). Useful when deciding where technical involvement will help most as the team changes.
- Charity Majors, [The Engineer/Manager Pendulum](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/hands-on-coding-managers/). On staying close to technical work as your responsibilities change.
