## What is Agile?

If you've ever been part of a project that spent months building something, only to discover at launch that the market had moved on or customers wanted something completely different, you'll understand why Agile exists. I've seen this happen more times than I'd like to admit, and it's painful every single time. (I've written about [my own journey with agile practices](/blog/category/management/agile-project-management-evolution) and how my thinking has evolved over the years, from following frameworks religiously to finding what actually works.)

Agile is fundamentally about learning fast and adapting quickly. Instead of betting everything on one massive release, you break work into chunks you can actually ship and get feedback on. Multiple checkpoints where you can course-correct, rather than driving blindly toward a destination that might not exist anymore.

Here's what I find most interesting: Agile isn't a set of rules. There's no Agile police checking whether your standups are done right. The [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/) is four sentences of priorities, not process:

- Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
- Working software over comprehensive documentation
- Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
- Responding to change over following a plan

Everything else (the sprints, the boards, the ceremonies) is scaffolding that teams built around those priorities. Which brings us to the two most durable pieces of scaffolding: Scrum and Kanban. They solve the same problem with two different clocks, and understanding that difference is most of what this post is about.

## Scrum: the fixed clock

I get asked all the time whether Scrum and Agile are the same thing. They're not, and the difference matters: Agile is the philosophy, the why. Scrum is one way to actually do it, the how, and it's deliberately prescriptive so a team can borrow structure while it develops its own judgment. The parts worth being strict about are the feedback loops; the rest you adapt to your context, and you should.

Scrum builds on two ideas. Empiricism: decide based on what you observe, not what you assumed three months ago. And lean thinking: cut what doesn't contribute. The framework admits upfront that you don't know everything at the start, and its whole structure (short cycles, frequent reprioritization, constant inspection) exists to make that admission safe.

### The team

A Scrum team is intentionally small, ten people at most, and I've found the best ones are five to seven (the "two pizza" heuristic holds up). Three roles:

- **Product owner**: the team's connection to reality. They own the backlog, keep it prioritized and clean (harder than it sounds), make sure everyone understands *why* something is being built, and decide when things ship. One critical rule I've learned the hard way: you need exactly ONE product owner. Teams get whiplash when multiple people fill this role with conflicting priorities. And note that product owner is not the same job as product manager, even when it's the same person.
- **Scrum master**: the process guardian, not a project manager. A coach who helps the team work better together, spots where things are stuck, gets them unstuck, and runs the ceremonies.
- **The development team**: everyone who builds the thing. Not just developers: testers, designers, ops, the whole crew. Self-organizing, cross-skilled enough that no single person is a bottleneck, and responsible for planning their own sprints based on what they've delivered before.

What makes these teams work is ownership. They define "done", they set the quality bar, they set the pace. Giving a team that much autonomy feels risky the first time, but what I've consistently observed is that trusted teams exceed what anyone thought was possible.

### The artifacts

Three shared pieces of information keep everyone aligned:

- **Product backlog**: the master list, owned by the product owner, alive and constantly reprioritized. Items that felt critical last month become irrelevant this month, and that is the system working, not failing.
- **Sprint backlog**: what the team committed to right now. Details can shift mid-sprint as you learn, but the sprint goal stays locked. You don't change what you're trying to achieve halfway through.
- **The increment**: what you actually built and could ship. Some teams release every sprint; others batch releases. Neither is wrong, but the longer you wait between real releases, the higher the odds you've built something the market no longer wants.

<Callout type='info'>
**Sprint**: a fixed time box, usually two weeks, in which the team commits to a specific goal. Keep the length consistent: that consistency is what makes your delivery data comparable sprint over sprint.
</Callout>

### The ceremonies

Scrum's meetings have a bad reputation, and honestly, some of it is earned. My advice: run all of them for a couple of sprints, then keep what helps and change what feels like box-checking.

1. **Sprint planning**: the team picks the sprint goal and pulls in the stories that serve it. Everyone leaves knowing what will be delivered and roughly how.
2. **The daily scrum**: fifteen minutes, same time, same place. Its real job is surfacing blockers and aligning on the next 24 hours. When it degrades into people reading their calendars aloud (and it will), change the format rather than abandoning it.
3. **Sprint review**: demo the increment to stakeholders, gather reactions, and let the product owner rework the backlog with what everyone learned.
4. **Retrospective**: the team inspects itself rather than the product: what to keep, what to drop, what to try. Skipping retros is how process debt accumulates.

Alongside the ceremonies runs **backlog refinement** (you may know it by its older name, grooming): the ongoing work of keeping the list prioritized and ready, feeding in what the product owner learns from users and the team. It isn't a formal Scrum event, and treating it as a continuous habit rather than yet another meeting is usually the healthier reading.

### The values

Scrum names five values: commitment, courage, focus, openness, and respect. That can sound like poster material, but there's an operational core to each: commit only to what you can finish, feel safe questioning the status quo and being honest about delays, protect the sprint's focus, keep progress and blockers visible, and treat every contribution as part of the win. Teams that live these need very little enforcement of the mechanics.

## Kanban: the continuous clock

While Scrum dominates the Agile conversation, Kanban has quietly become one of my favorite approaches for certain teams. Its roots are in 1940s Toyota, long before software: supermarkets stocked only what customers actually bought, right when they needed it, and Toyota translated that into cards (*kanban*, "visual signal") passed between factory stations to request materials. Make only what's needed, exactly when it's needed.

Software teams apply the same principle to work in progress instead of inventory. And the barrier to entry is wonderfully low: no new roles, no ceremonies to schedule. A board, some cards, and one rule. You could start tomorrow.

### The board and the cards

Everything revolves around the board: the single source of truth for the team's work. The basic workflow is three columns, To Do, In Progress, Done, and you extend it to match your actual process (a Review column, a Ready-for-QA column, whatever reflects reality). Each work item is a card carrying enough context that anyone can see who owns it, what it is, and where it's stuck. The point of all this visibility is fast identification of blockers and dependencies, not decoration.

### The one rule that matters: WIP limits

The thing that turns a board from a to-do list into a system is the work-in-progress limit. Multitasking kills efficiency: the more items in flight, the more context switching, and the slower everything lands. So you cap each column.

A concrete example: cap Review at 2. That sounds brutally low, and that's the point. Developers would rather write new code than review someone else's, so reviews queue and rot. A full Review column blocks new work from entering, which forces the team to finish reviews before starting anything new. The limit converts a cultural nag ("please review my PR") into a mechanical property of the system.

The metric that falls out of this is **cycle time**: how long a card takes from started to shipped. Consistent, short cycle times make forecasting honest, and because any card can ship the moment it lands in Done, Kanban pairs naturally with continuous delivery. No release ceremony, no waiting for the sprint boundary.

## Picking between them

After working with both, I've stopped thinking of this as Scrum versus Kanban and started asking four questions about the team:

| The question | Points to Scrum | Points to Kanban |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the work arrive as a roadmap or as a stream? | Roadmap: features you can plan a goal around | Stream: support, platform work, unpredictable requests |
| Does the team need protection from mid-cycle churn? | Yes: the sprint boundary is a shield | No: priorities genuinely change daily and that's fine |
| What should improve first? | Estimation and forecasting (velocity) | Throughput and responsiveness (cycle time) |
| How much process can the team absorb right now? | Roles and ceremonies help a team that wants structure | One board and one rule suit a team allergic to meetings |

Two honest caveats from experience. First, these blend: plenty of good teams run Scrum ceremonies over a WIP-limited board and call it whatever they like. The frameworks are starting points, not identities. Second, whichever you pick, the failure mode is the same: following the mechanics while ignoring the feedback loops they exist to create.
