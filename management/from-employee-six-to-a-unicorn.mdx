One of our founders sold his cars so we could make payroll. Years later, the company was valued at more than a billion dollars. I was there for both.

After five years at [SAP](https://www.sap.com/), I joined [Beamery](https://beamery.com/) as employee number six, one of the founding engineers. I wanted the things that make startups appealing: interesting work, freedom, ownership, and a share in the upside if we built something valuable.

Over the following decade, I watched a handful of people become a company of more than four hundred. I saw early colleagues leave, built an AI team inside the business, and experienced the excitement of becoming a unicorn. I also watched repeated layoffs wear away the confidence that had taken years to build.

By then, I had plenty of reasons to understand why someone would choose a different kind of job. I still wanted to build another startup. What had changed was how I thought about the people, the business and my own place in it.

I kept coming back to a question: if I had the chance to help shape a company from the beginning again, what would I do differently?

## At the beginning, we were all figuring it out

We were cramped into a small office, excited to be doing the startup thing. It was our first time building a company. We had all seen the movies and had some version of that picture in our heads: the tiny team, the chaos, the stories we would tell when it all worked out. We were already talking about how we would remember those days once we were successful.

There was a lot of fun in it. Some people had taken a chance on us, and we were going to see what we could make together. We still had to work out whether the idea could work and whether anyone would pay for it. We were learning about the product and the business at the same time.

The pressure became much more real as the money started running out. Keeping the company going meant finding enough traction to raise another round. Selling became an obsession. Sell, sell, sell. We needed somebody to take a chance on the product we were building, just as others had taken a chance on us.

Eventually, we landed our first customer. We got feedback, improved what we were doing, and won our first large enterprise account. That helped us raise Series A. It also gave the engineering work a clearer direction: we had customers whose experience could tell us what to build next.

We could start adding people around the original team. That felt like progress, and it brought a question we hadn't had to answer in the small office: who would get to lead the company we were becoming?

## After Series A, who gets to grow?

There still wasn't much hierarchy, but we could feel the need for more structure. Leadership started looking for people who had done this before.

I remember plenty of relatively junior hires early on. Keeping salary costs down was an important consideration. People received equity too, so they had a stake in what we were building. But we hadn't started with a team full of people who had already led companies through the next few stages of growth.

I understood the attraction of an experienced head of engineering or VP. We were learning while trying to keep the business moving. Someone who knew what the next stage required could help us get there.

For people on the original team, those hires raised a different question. They had taken on work before there were proper roles for it. They had helped create the opportunity. Now that there was a larger job to do, why wasn't there a path for them to grow into it? That tension reached across the company. A sensible decision to bring in experience could still leave an early colleague wondering where they fitted.

Around Series B, some of the core team started leaving. I could see people losing motivation when they weren't getting the responsibility or progression they wanted, and couldn't see enough support to get there. The company was getting bigger while their own future in it felt smaller.

My position was different. I had specialist expertise and a distinct place in the business. I felt trusted and had room to pursue work that interested me. I wasn't being passed over in the way some colleagues felt they were. I could see their frustration while still having opportunities of my own.

### Lesson 1: Help early colleagues grow with the company

I tell the people I manage that my job is to help them get where they want to be as quickly as possible. I want to know what they are aiming for while the company is creating new roles, when there is still a chance to shape them.

Looking back, I would have wanted us to be much more deliberate about how experienced hires helped the people already there develop. The new person might be right for the job. The colleague who wanted that responsibility still needs a future they can see and help preparing for it. Equity gives someone a stake in a possible financial outcome; it doesn't answer their question about who they can become inside the company.

I want the people I'm hiring now to have a chance to grow into the leadership roles we'll need. I look at how they make decisions, respond to unfamiliar work and help others develop, as well as what they can deliver immediately. When we bring in additional experience, I want it to help them prepare for that responsibility too.

I'm also putting much more experience into that first team, and I'm willing to pay more for it. I want people who can [work effectively with AI](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature) and judge the result: what is worth pursuing, what needs checking, and when something is ready for a customer. I'm willing to spend more each month if it helps us get something valuable into a customer's hands sooner.

## As we scaled, the culture changed

As we raised more and hired more, the immediate feeling of running out of money eased. Keeping people motivated became harder. The company was succeeding at growing, but some of what had made it enjoyable to work there was becoming difficult to recognise.

The early culture lived in the people I worked with every day. We had some written values from the beginning, but the ones that felt most real were unwritten. They showed up in our expectations of each other, the room we had to try things, and the sense of being in it together. We had shared the uncertainty of getting the company started. That experience influenced how we worked without needing much explanation.

When early colleagues left, some of that went with them. Replacing their responsibilities didn't automatically replace their influence. They were the people who could show a new joiner how we approached the work, often without thinking of it as teaching anyone about culture. Some of the people who felt they had run out of room were also carrying the habits we wanted to keep.

Meanwhile, the managers we brought in were shaping the daily experience of the growing teams. Some came from large enterprise companies and brought the processes they knew. We needed more coordination; I understood that. What frustrated me was how much of their previous way of working seemed to arrive with them, without enough questioning of whether it fitted the company we were building.

For someone joining at that stage, their manager's approach was the culture. They hadn't been in the early office with us. The habits of the people making decisions around them now mattered more than our founding story.

There wasn't one process I could point to as the moment everything changed. It was the accumulation: more structure, more steps, more effort to get the same things done. We were adding people and experience while losing some of the speed that had made the work exciting.

As we scaled, we tried to put more of our values on paper. That made sense: new people hadn't shared the early experiences, and we wanted to describe what mattered to us. But I remember an expensive two-day offsite with the whole leadership team, with workshops to come up with a new set of values.

At one point, we ended up with more than twenty values. “Be the change you seek.” “Act with kindness.” I remember the phrases, and I remember the posters in meeting rooms. I didn't see those posters shaping our everyday work in the way the early, unwritten values had.

### Lesson 2: Pay attention to who carries the culture

When we hire managers now, I want to pay as much attention to how they work with people as to the experience on their CV. I had watched their habits shape the company more than the words on the posters. I want a short list of values a new colleague can recognise in the team they join, starting with behaviour already worth keeping.

## What people were joining for

In a small team, breakfasts, dinners and offsites were relatively affordable. They also felt personal. People were working hard and taking a chance on the company; some might have earned more somewhere established. Spending time and money on the team could feel like recognition of that commitment. A modest gesture went a long way when we all knew what it had taken to get there.

As Beamery grew, the perks grew too. I remember First Friday off and expensive offsites in different countries. The spending became much larger, but I didn't feel it brought the same appreciation or connection. My impression was that we had started attracting some people more through the perks than through an interest in what we were building.

Later, when the business was under pressure and perks started disappearing, people left. I couldn't know every person's reasons, especially with layoffs and uncertainty around them. But I felt that, for some, the company had lost the thing that attracted them in the first place. It made me question what we were asking people to join for.

### Lesson 3: Be deliberate about what attracts people

I still want to spend money on the team and offer benefits people value as we grow. Those benefits are part of the job we offer, and removing them changes that offer. I want to choose things we can sustain at a larger size and through a harder year, and understand whether people still value them as much as we assume.

When hiring, I want to understand what interests someone about the work, the customers and the responsibility they would have. I want to give them a clear picture of those things alongside the package. Perks can help make a good job more attractive. I want someone to have reasons to choose the job that would still matter if we couldn't afford the next overseas offsite.

## A startup inside the startup

Around the time of Series C, I got the green light to build the AI and research team, focused on applied AI. We had found product-market fit, with annual recurring revenue in the tens of millions. The business needed to stay ahead and make the product meaningfully different. Leadership trusted me to take that work on.

For me, it was a chance to build a startup inside the startup. There was interesting technical work to do, a business reason to invest in it, and room to create our own working culture. I could do something about the loss of freedom that had been frustrating me.

I remember the company being on Confluence and Jira while we used Notion. People questioned why our team was using something different, and I pushed for it. It was a small, visible decision about how we wanted to work. I wanted the team to know we could make those choices and stand behind them.

That helped give us an identity within the larger company. People had a say in the environment they were working in, and we did work I was proud of. It also gave me a clearer understanding of what I enjoyed about leading a team, beyond choosing the technical direction.

### Lesson 4: Give trusted people room to make decisions

The applied AI team needed room to investigate ideas before we knew whether they would be useful. That freedom was part of what I enjoyed about the job, and I wanted the people I managed to have it too.

Working together taught me how people approached unfamiliar problems, explained tradeoffs and recognised when they needed help. The difficult part was letting their judgment count when I would have chosen another approach. I could easily keep stepping in until they had very little left to decide.

That freedom also required something from me: enough context to make the decision, time for a difficult question and support when things went wrong. If I wanted people to challenge my approach, I had to listen and be willing to change it. Those expectations have become much more explicit in [how we work together at Mav9](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design).

Most of my team at Mav9 came from my previous team at Beamery. I already knew their work and trusted them to run with things without being micromanaged. We had built relationships over time, and I wanted to build with those people again. Those relationships are one of the most valuable things I carried out of that decade.

## The context couldn't stay in our heads

Another thing I learned at Beamery was how important documentation is while the team is still small. In the early office, we shared a lot of context simply by being there together. It was easy to assume that talking about something meant the team knew it. As we grew, I came to care much more about what someone could understand without having been in those conversations.

That matters even more in remote and asynchronous teams. I want someone to be able to pick up the work while the person who knows its history is offline. They need to understand what we decided, why we chose it and what is still open. Otherwise, the freedom to work independently comes with a dependency on someone else's availability.

### Lesson 5: Build the documentation habit early

I now push for a structured home for that context from the beginning: product decisions, how the system works, who is responsible for what, and the ways we work together. I think of it as the team's shared memory. Someone joining should be able to build on what we have learned without needing an early employee to retell it all.

This is where I push back on the Agile Manifesto's “working software over comprehensive documentation” when it becomes a reason to postpone writing things down. The manifesto still values documentation. But in a remote team, leaving enough context for another person to work independently is part of building working software. I don't want it to be whatever we get around to after shipping.

I would start with decisions written where people will look for them, with enough reasoning to understand them later. Someone needs to keep that explanation current and make clear what is still open. I would establish the habit before hiring made it urgent; waiting until everyone was asking the same questions would mean we were already paying for its absence.

## Later, growth stopped being the whole story

By our Series D in December 2022, Beamery had reached a valuation of more than a billion dollars. Going from employee six to working at a unicorn was extraordinary. We had customers, a much larger team and several funding rounds behind us. It felt like recognition of years of work.

There was a lot to be proud of, and I found it easy to let that pride become confidence about the future. We had come through so many earlier challenges. Reaching this stage made it tempting to think the business was on much firmer ground.

The pandemic had already arrived before Series C and D. We were reaching those milestones while the conditions around the business were changing. Growth and uncertainty overlapped; the funding announcements were only one part of what was happening.

Later, the downturn and pressure on sales brought a very different experience of the company. There were layoffs, then more layoffs, then more again. I watched morale fall as people went through that repeatedly. After seeing the company expand for so long, it was difficult to take in how quickly we could find ourselves shrinking.

### Lesson 6: A funding round doesn't make the company secure

The repetition is what stayed with me. After one difficult round of cuts, there is a desire to believe the company can settle and move forward. Another round makes that harder, and the possibility of it happening again becomes part of how everyone looks at the future. A financial decision happens quickly. Recovering the team's confidence takes much longer, and with repeated cuts people have to adjust again before they have settled from the last time.

The people who remained still had a product to develop and customers to serve. They had to absorb what had happened, adjust to who was left and keep investing energy in the next plan. I could still be proud of what we had built while feeling much less certain about where it was going. Watching the team go through that made me see their recovery as part of the work ahead of the business.

I had read too much into the funding announcement. The money gave us time, but we still had to sell enough, make good decisions about spending and cope with conditions we didn't control.

I also looked back differently at some of our bigger decisions. We had turned down acquisition offers and made acquisitions of our own. Some of those acquisitions, from my perspective, didn't deliver what we had hoped for. As the circumstances changed, I kept thinking about the choices we had made when the future looked more generous. Their consequences stayed with the business as its options became narrower.

I now spend more time on the less exciting versions of a plan. What would we do if it took longer, or if sales were slower than we hoped? I want to know what we could afford to keep doing and what we would have to change.

I also can't expect a team to commit to a new plan as though the previous one never happened. After repeated layoffs, people have good reasons to be wary. I need to take that seriously when asking them to put their effort into what comes next.

## AI did not shorten the sales cycle

Our team had been doing applied AI work for years when the surge of interest in it gave us more to consider for the product.

I saw customers want AI and be nervous about buying an AI-driven product at the same time. They could see possibilities in the technology while being unsure how to bring it into their organisation. They were interested, but they still had a lot to work through before approving a purchase.

I remember a lot of questionnaires. How could they use AI safely? How should they assess bias? What would an audit need to show? We were answering questions about the product while customers were still working out what their own evaluation and approval process should look like. Sometimes the process around buying was as unfamiliar as the technology itself.

Their caution made sense. We were building for talent teams, where fairness mattered to how the product would be used, and the people assessing it had responsibilities of their own.

We put substantial work into bias audits, including independent audits. Customers could examine what had been tested, what the results showed and what they could reasonably conclude from them, alongside our own explanations.

ISO/IEC 42001, published in 2023, sets out requirements for an AI management system. I see it as a useful place to start a conversation about how we manage AI. We still have to answer questions about the particular product and how a customer intends to use it.

### Lesson 7: Allow time for customers to get comfortable buying

Enterprise sales were already lengthy. AI added questions that customers themselves were still learning how to answer. We could move the product forward while a buying decision remained some distance away. The urgency inside our company didn't make their process move at the speed we needed. That was especially difficult when we were looking to new technical work to help the business find its next direction.

Hiring senior people and using AI may help us build faster now. It won't remove the work a customer has to do before buying. I want to know early who needs to approve the product, what evidence they will need, and how we can help them assess it.

We have to keep paying for the work while we wait. Customers can have good reasons for taking their time, but we still need revenue.

## The financial upside, and the wait for cash

I had also joined for the financial upside. As employee six, I was early enough to help shape the company itself. I wanted the years I put into it to lead to a meaningful financial return too.

What I understood much less clearly was how long it could take before ownership became money someone could use. The company could raise another round and continue hiring while the question of when an employee could sell remained open. Funding the business and giving shareholders a way to receive cash were separate events.

The first secondary opportunity I saw at Beamery came at Series C, already years after I had joined. It allowed some existing shares to be sold while the company was still private, without waiting for an acquisition or a public listing.

Access was limited. From memory, only a handful of people across the company were able to take part. Saying that the company had done a secondary could make it sound like a milestone for the whole team. In practice, its significance depended on whether you were included. Even being there almost from the beginning had meant years of work before seeing that first opportunity.

The unicorn announcement came later, at Series D. I remember the excitement of thinking about what our shares might be worth at that valuation. We had helped build something investors valued at more than a billion dollars. It was natural to start doing the arithmetic and imagine what that could mean for our lives.

### Lesson 8: Paper wealth isn't money in the bank

The funding announcement didn't mean employees could sell at the values they were imagining. It didn't tell us when another secondary would happen or who could participate. I knew that and still got caught up in the possibilities.

Later setbacks changed how I thought about those numbers. A valuation from a funding round could remain the number people remembered while sales and the outlook for the business became less certain. I had to stop treating an earlier headline as a fixed expectation of what my equity would eventually deliver.

It also changed how I compared joining at the beginning with joining after several rounds. They are different financial decisions. Later employees can have valuable equity, but I wouldn't assume that their opportunity resembles the one offered to the first few hires. I would want to understand the actual ownership on offer and what would need to happen for me to receive money from it. A company's size or reputation can't answer that for me.

For a long time, I put Beamery first. I treated the company's success as if it would automatically become my own. That was a mistake. The company could reach its milestones while my financial outcome still depended on a separate set of conditions.

I also became more aware of how incentives can diverge after some people have cashed out. Someone who has already received a meaningful payout can be comfortable with the business running on terms that suit them. Someone who received very little may still be chasing the outcome that makes their commitment financially worthwhile. The same company can represent a comfortable lifestyle for one person and a promise of future reward for another. Being in it together doesn't mean everyone still needs the same thing from it.

That made me more willing to be selfish about my own financial outcome. I want the upside to be clear, and I want to understand the conditions attached to it. Loyalty to a company shouldn't mean leaving my own interests unexamined. The years I put into building it are years of my life too.

I now want equity on top of a base salary I'm happy with. I won't compromise that base pay in exchange for a possible payout later. I still want meaningful ownership in what I'm building, but I need to be happy with the job even if I cannot sell those shares for years.

## Why I wanted to do it again, as CTO

After all of that, I still wanted the experience of getting something off the ground with a small team. I knew how much I enjoyed building, and I wanted a bigger part in the decisions that shaped the company around the product. The next opportunity had to make sense with everything I had learned along the way.

When I joined Mav9, funding was already in place to cover at least three years under our plan, including hiring a full team. I had seen what running short of money did to a company and the people in it. I wanted enough room to build without immediately depending on the next round.

The work was greenfield again. I could get back to building, make early technical choices and shape the product as we learned. Building the AI team had given me a chance to create one part of a larger company. This time I wanted to be involved while those choices were still being made across the business.

We also had design partners and sales channels already in place. At Beamery, I had seen HR teams want the product without having the budget to buy it. At Mav9, I expected finance buyers to have more budget for the problems we wanted to solve. There were people we could work with on real problems, ways to reach customers, and a reason to believe those customers could pay.

And I really liked the founding team. The trust was mutual. These were people I wanted to work with through difficult decisions as well as exciting ones.

I was joining early again, this time as CTO. More of the decisions I had watched play out at Beamery would be mine to make. I wanted the people joining me to have the opportunities I had valued, and fewer reasons to feel they had run out of room.

My [first year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-nobody-handed-me-a-manual) also included payroll, VAT returns and security policies. I had chosen the responsibility for building a company around the product. I was learning how much work that involved.

## A few useful reads

- GitLab, [The importance of a handbook-first approach](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/handbook-first/). On making shared documentation part of how a team communicates, starting while it is still small.
- [Manifesto for Agile Software Development](https://agilemanifesto.org/). The original wording values documentation too; the priority it gives working software is where I want teams to think carefully about what enables people to keep building it.
- Felix Carmona, [Scaling Trust](https://felixcarmona.com/scaling-trust-transitioning-into-engineering-management/). On giving people room to decide and the support a manager owes them alongside that freedom.
- Ben Link, [How to Build Trust with Engineers](https://dev.to/linkbenjamin/how-to-build-trust-with-engineers-a-guide-for-execs-1j86). On listening to disagreement and following through when people raise concerns.
- Ben Horowitz, [The Hard Thing About Hard Things](https://a16z.com/books/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things/). The book I'd put alongside the downturn in this story, when the business still needs decisions and the team has already been through a lot.
- Holloway, [The Holloway Guide to Equity Compensation](https://www.holloway.com/g/equity-compensation). The reference I wish I had read before signing my first option grant: vesting, exercise windows, preferences and what each one does to the number on the headline.
