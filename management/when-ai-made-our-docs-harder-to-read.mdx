We started with a vibe-coded prototype and tried to plan the whole product around it. Break it into modules, define the personas, work through what each part should do. We wanted to go all the way from the prototype to a proper plan.

It was too much. We brought AI into the writing, and the documentation grew. The result was long, unreadable documents about a product we were still figuring out. We had made it easier to produce the material without making it easier for the team to use it.

That was part of [how we kept changing our planning process](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year), and it frustrated me because [Beamery had taught me to push for documentation early](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn#lesson-5-build-the-documentation-habit-early). In an asynchronous team, written context lets someone carry on without waiting for the person who made the decision. I wanted a shared place for that understanding. Documents nobody could get through were failing at the very thing I needed them for.

We now have a few rules for AI-assisted writing. They put the responsibility back on the person sharing the document, including me.

## I need to understand what I put my name on

AI is useful when I need to untangle an explanation, test an assumption or find a question I have missed. It can help me get to an answer as well as help me explain one. I want us to keep using it for that.

But if I share a design, I should be able to explain the decisions in it. Why this approach? What are we assuming? What is still unknown? The document might be beautifully written and still contain an answer I have never thought through.

Our rule is that someone has to understand, verify and stand behind what we publish. That includes diagrams and recommendations, not just factual claims. “AI wrote that part” cannot be where my explanation ends. I need to work out whether the part belongs there and whether I agree with it.

I ask for the same [ownership when AI helps us write code](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature). Putting my name on a document carries a similar responsibility to the people who will rely on it.

## Someone still has to read it

The time AI saves me writing can become time I ask everyone else to spend reading. A long document has to earn that attention. If the important decision is buried halfway down, the reader has to do work I could have done before sharing it.

So editing is part of the job. Put the important information first. Remove the repeated explanation. Cut the generic introduction that tells this team things it already knows. If three bullets explain the decision, they do not need to become three pages.

A complex design may still need detail. I want the reader to understand the main decision quickly, then find the reasoning, assumptions and supporting material when they need them. Concise does not mean leaving out the thing another engineer needs to change the system safely.

We also encourage diagrams, flows and tables where they make an explanation easier to follow. I can ask AI to help produce those too. I still need to check that the picture represents the system we mean to describe.

## Leave enough for someone to carry on

The test we use is whether someone else can understand the document and move forward without its author. That is what I need from documentation in a team that works asynchronously.

Before sharing, I need to read it with that person in mind. Can they tell what has been decided? Are the assumptions visible? Can they find the context behind a choice? Have I left them a question to resolve, or accidentally presented an unanswered question as settled?

That gives me a practical way to respond to the unreadable documents we produced. I can take a draft, check its claims, remove what does not help the reader, and make the decision and remaining questions easy to find. AI can help with that editing. I am still responsible for deciding when the document is ready for someone else's time.
