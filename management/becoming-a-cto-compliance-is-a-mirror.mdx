I expected compliance to be paperwork: documents to write, screenshots to collect, and eventually something reassuring to put on the website. A year of working through SOC 2 and ISO 27001 has made it much more personal than that.

Someone would ask how a control worked. I would find the policy, read the sentence I had written, and then go looking for the thing the sentence described. Sometimes the two matched. Sometimes I found a plan I had mistaken for a fact.

## Reading my own promises

One procedure said that approvals were dismissed when new commits arrived on a pull request. The setting was off.

Another said that a departing engineer's laptop would be wiped within twenty-four hours. Nobody had left yet, and I couldn't point to a record showing that we had put that step into operation. An untested procedure was sitting in the same collection as the things we actually did.

The most uncomfortable example was a custom control that claimed more than we had built. The evidence behind it covered one component and one successful test. The claim in the policy was system-wide.

I had written these things with confidence because they sounded like the company we intended to build. Once they were in a policy, they looked like descriptions of the company we already had. The person reading them had no reason to know the difference.

That was the part I had to own. The gap did not disappear because the intended design was sensible or because I could explain what we meant to do next.

## A shorter document can still leave a real gap

We removed a sampled post-merge review from a change procedure before the procedure was approved. It was an additional commitment we were not going to carry out every month. Leaving it in would have given us another promise to break.

I have become much more careful about adding those sentences. It's easy to make a procedure sound thorough by adding another review, another sign-off, or another report. Someone then has to do that work, and someone has to notice when it stops happening.

But shortening the document is only part of the answer. If a control is required, or is how we have agreed to address a real risk, deleting the sentence does not remove the obligation. We need to implement it, agree an appropriate alternative, or make an explicit decision about the remaining risk. I don't want “keep the policy realistic” to become an excuse for leaving the system inadequate.

That control made the distinction concrete. By September, refreshing the screenshot from March would not have answered the underlying question. I put the choice to the team: implement the control properly or retire the claim. Retirement would need a reason we could defend, including what happened to the risk the control was meant to address.

Putting the choice on the table did not complete the work. It did make the unfinished work harder to hide behind a document.

## Making evidence available

We also had a much simpler problem. Our corrective-action records were complete, but they were behind a login the auditor did not have. I could see them; the person checking our work could not.

We now export them. That is a small administrative change, but it exposed another assumption I had been making: because I knew where the evidence lived, I thought I had supplied it.

I need to be able to hand someone enough context to understand what a record demonstrates. A screenshot of one successful test has a narrower meaning than a claim that a control works across the system. A completed record that nobody reviewing the control can access does not help them check it.

That has changed the questions I ask when gathering evidence. What does this actually show? What does it leave untested? Can the person reading it understand those limits without me standing beside them?

## The useful part of being checked

Three findings from our last review were things I had half-known and had not written down anywhere. They are now on a list with owners and dates. That is a more useful result than another polished policy.

The auditor could read my words without supplying all the context I carried in my head. They needed to see what existed. Their questions sent me back to check things I had been too close to notice.

An audit is one opportunity to do this checking. The day-to-day work still belongs to us. We can inspect settings, test procedures, and review evidence before someone else asks. Those checks need to catch gaps between formal reviews.

I still find parts of compliance tedious. I also find it harder now to dismiss the work as overhead. It has repeatedly put a sentence I wrote next to something we had actually built and made me deal with the difference.

## A few useful reads

- Steven Shorrock, [The Varieties of Human Work](https://humanisticsystems.com/2016/12/05/the-varieties-of-human-work/). Useful language for the difference between what a procedure says, what we tell someone, and what happens in practice.
- Phil Venables, [Control Reliability Engineering](https://www.philvenables.com/post/control-reliability-engineering-cre-applying-sre-principles-to-cybersecurity-controls). A practical way to think about checking whether a security control keeps working after its first successful test.
- GitLab, [The importance of a handbook-first approach](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/handbook-first/). Worth reading when deciding whether documentation is part of how people work or something maintained separately for a review.
