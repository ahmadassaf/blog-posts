After spending a decade at one startup, I had a much clearer idea of what I wanted from the next one. I thought carefully about the team and the opportunity to build. I had thought much less about the company work that would come with being a founder.

## Choosing to do it again

I joined my first startup as employee six and was part of its growth to more than 400 people and unicorn status. I built its AI and research team. I also saw early colleagues leave, watched the company change as it hired managers, and lived through repeated rounds of layoffs. The company could be worth a great deal on paper while people inside it were worried about their jobs.

I still wanted to build again. I liked the early work: figuring out a problem with a small group, trying something, and learning from the people who might use it. The experience had changed what I needed to know before committing to another company. I tell the longer story in [Ten Years in Startups: Why I'm Building Again](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn).

When I chose to join Mav9, we had funding that, under our plan at the time, covered at least three years with a full team. That mattered because I wanted enough time to build with the people we intended to hire. Having seen funding rounds followed by layoffs, I also knew how much a plan could change.

We had design partners and sales channels already in place. I wanted a way to get our work in front of prospective customers early. In the first startup, finding customers had given the engineers direction as well as helping the company raise money. A willing design partner would not answer every product question, but they gave us somewhere to start testing our assumptions.

I paid more attention to who would buy the product, too. Selling to HR had taught me how much budget and buying authority mattered. I expected finance to give us a different opportunity, but that expectation still needed testing with actual buyers. Interest in a demo does not tell me whose budget a purchase will come from or who else has to approve it. Those are conversations I would want alongside the technical discovery.

The work was greenfield, and I would join early enough to influence how the company was built. I liked and trusted the founding team. After years in a much larger organisation, I wanted to be close to those decisions again. Someone looking for a more established role might choose differently; I wanted the room to shape one.

### What I would settle before taking the role

Looking back, I would use those reasons to make the conversation more concrete. I would ask the founders to walk me through the hiring plan and the assumptions behind the funding available to support it. I would want to know which prospective customers we could actually work with, what they had agreed to contribute, and how we expected to reach a buying decision. “We have customers interested” can cover very different levels of commitment.

For the CTO role, I would also write down the responsibilities beyond engineering that the founders already knew somebody had to take. Where there was no owner, I would want an explicit temporary arrangement and a point at which we would revisit it. I did plenty of that work willingly. The trouble came when I kept carrying it without checking what the team needed from me instead.

I would separate my own financial decision from my excitement about the company. At the first startup, secondary sales eventually gave some people access to money from their shares, but the valuation alone did not do that. My preference now is for base pay I am comfortable with and equity on top. I want to understand the possible upside without needing it to justify the everyday arrangement.

## Taking the role

On the first day the title meant one thing to me: when it breaks, I am the person responsible. What I had not worked out was how many different things “it” would be.

I came from research and machine learning. I had never run production infrastructure, been the name on a security policy or submitted a VAT return. A founding CTO does not get to wait for a specialist before the work is done, so the useful question early on is which parts you take yourself for now, and what would have to be true for you to hand each one over.

### How we did it at Mav9

There was no job description. There was a founding team, a product to ship, and a list of things that needed someone to pick them up, and I picked up a lot of them. I describe that list in [Nobody Handed Me a Manual](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-nobody-handed-me-a-manual).

Something I took on because nobody else could do it could stay mine long after that reason had gone. The thing to record for each one is why it is yours and what would end that. The [Individual Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-individual-habits) chapter has the two lists I now keep for this.

## The first team

My first decision was who to build with. I chose people I had worked with before. I knew how they worked and what they would say when they disagreed with me, and I could give one of them a problem and turn to something else.

The first startup had made a different early hiring trade-off. We brought in junior people, kept costs down and offered equity. As the company grew, it needed more experienced leadership. Some of the early colleagues felt that the new responsibilities were going to people hired above them, without enough help to grow into those roles themselves. Around Series B, some began leaving. That was not my own experience: my specialist work gave me opportunities and trust that others did not always have.

I want the people who join early to have a future in the company they help build. That requires conversations about what they want to do next, and opportunities to practise it while there is still someone to support them. Hiring an experienced manager may be necessary. I would want that person's job to include developing the people already there, rather than treating every new responsibility as another vacancy to fill from outside.

There are two things to check when you hire from your own history. One is whether people who were right for a company of hundreds are right for a much earlier stage. Some of what made them valuable before matters less now, and some of what matters now they have never had to do. The other is whether you are relying on that history to carry information it cannot carry. Years of working together tell someone how I think. They do not tell them what they can decide in this company.

### How we did it at Mav9

Most of the first engineers had been on my team at my last company. I wanted experienced people who could take a problem and run with it, and I expected AI to multiply what a small team of them could do. That part held up; I wrote about it in [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

Paying more for that experience meant spending more at the beginning. My bet was that a small team with the judgement to frame a problem, use AI well and recognise when an approach needed changing could get useful work in front of customers sooner. That was the trade-off I chose for this product and this stage.

The part I got wrong was assuming trust was the whole arrangement. I had done much less to make sure they knew what they could decide and what they needed from me. The fix came later and is in [Group Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits).

One line from our values has turned out to be a good hiring rule for a CTO: know your talent, hire your weaknesses. I hired specialists because they knew things I did not. The current hiring process is in [Processes](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-processes).

## Setting a direction

Somebody has to say where the centre of the system is. Early on, you can do a lot of that through who you hire: bring in people who can each build one part of the picture, and tell them how the parts meet.

A direction set once does not keep. A customer asks for something, a part turns out harder than it looked, or two people's reasonable choices meet in the middle. Each of those needs someone to work out what it means for the rest of the plan. If nobody does, people make local decisions that are each sensible and do not always add up.

### How we did it at Mav9

I wanted a knowledge graph at the centre of the product, with the agents, interfaces and integrations reading from and writing to that one model. I hired for each part, laid the foundations and let people run with the rest.

It worked for a while. Then I found out late that some decisions did not fit together, because I had assumed the first direction would hold. The engineers needed clear priorities more than they needed more architecture from me. That story is in [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

The technical choices I wanted settled early, such as service ownership, configuration, tests and releases, are written up in [The Engineering Foundations I Want in Place Early](/blog/category/management/setting-the-engineering-foundations).

For a CTO, the company direction has to reach those choices. A customer conversation can change which part of the system matters next, or reveal that an assumption two engineers share is wrong. If I keep that information in founder conversations, people will continue making reasonable decisions against an older picture. I need to explain what changed and work through the consequences with the people who know the implementation. Drawing the first architecture does not finish that work.

## From a prototype to real customers

A prototype is enough to start asking what needs validating. It is a weak basis for specifying every part of the finished product. The plan changes once real data arrives, so I would leave room for that from the start.

### How we did it at Mav9

We started from a vibe-coded prototype and tried to plan the whole product from it: modules, personas, long documents. It was too much. When design partners gave us real data, things looked very different from the tidy world of the prototype, and we shortened our planning cycle to learn faster. The full sequence is in [We Kept Trying to Plan Everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year).

I would now ask what a prototype has actually taught us before using it to commit the team. A working screen may help us discuss a customer's workflow while telling us very little about the data we will receive, the exceptions we will need to handle or how the product fits their existing work. Those unknowns belong in the next piece of discovery. Giving each one a detailed specification before we have seen real use only makes the assumption harder to notice.

We build for finance, so we also decided in the first year to pursue SOC 2 and ISO 27001, while the product and the team were still taking shape. Some of the controls I wrote down then described what I wanted us to have; see [Processes](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-processes).

## The work that is not engineering

A founder does whatever the company needs that nobody else is placed to do. For a CTO this can be a surprising share of the week, and it competes with the team for the same hours.

### How we did it at Mav9

The other founders are in Germany and I am in the UK. Setting up the UK entity fell to me, and so did the office, the pension provider, health insurance, payroll and VAT returns. I also built the first front end, because we had no front-end engineer.

When the admin and the team wanted the same hour, the admin usually won. I told myself that payroll going out on time was what kept the company moving. It helped, but the team needed more from me than that. When we could hire for the front end I handed it over, and that is still the clearest example I have of what a finished handover frees up. See [My First Year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much).
