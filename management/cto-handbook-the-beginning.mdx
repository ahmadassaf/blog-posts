Imagine you have incorporated your startup. You have an idea you believe in, you have raised money, and now you can get to work. There are people you want to hire and a product you can already picture. You open a document to plan the first week. What goes in it?

I would probably start drawing the system. That is familiar work for me. But before I turn that drawing into jobs and months of engineering, I want to know who we can build with on the customer side, what we need to learn from them, and what each founder is taking responsibility for.

That is the starting point for this chapter. The company in this scenario is imagined; the examples of what I tried, got wrong and would repeat come from my own experience. [About this handbook](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook) has the story of why I wanted to build again.

## What does the funding give you time to do?

You have money in the bank and a hiring plan. I would sit down with the other founders and walk through the assumptions connecting the two. How many people does the plan allow for? What are we expecting to have learned or built before we need to make another funding decision? Which parts depend on customers moving at the speed we hope they will?

When I chose to build again, our plan gave us at least three years with a full team. I wanted time to build with the people we intended to hire. I had also lived through funding rounds followed by layoffs, so I knew how much those assumptions could change.

For the CTO, those questions affect the first technical commitments. A hire, a long integration or a product promise can consume time we meant to use for something else. I want the founders to agree what the first team is trying to establish, so I can explain why we are spending that time and revisit the plan when we learn something different.

## Find someone to build with

Suppose a prospective customer likes the idea. The next conversation I would want is about a piece of their actual work. Who does it? What do they use today? What data could they show us, and who could spend time trying what we build?

We had design partners and sales channels in place when we started. That gave us access to people who might use the product, though we still had to find out how it would fit their work. At my first startup, customer conversations had given the engineers direction as well as helping the company raise money.

I would make the commitment concrete. “We have customers interested” might mean someone enjoyed a demo; it might mean a team is ready to work with us using its own data. Those lead to very different plans. I would want to know what the partner can contribute and what we have promised in return before scheduling engineering around it.

I also want the buying conversation happening alongside the technical one. Selling to HR taught me how much budget and authority matter. I expected finance to be a different opportunity, but I still needed to test that expectation with actual buyers. Who would pay, from which budget, and who else would need to agree? A good demo leaves those questions open.

## Give the first work an owner

Back in your first-week document, include the work needed to get the company running. Payroll needs arranging, customers have security questions and the product needs building. In a founding team, expect to take responsibility for work beyond your speciality. Divide that work between you so everyone knows who is doing it.

The other founders are in Germany and I am in the UK. I set up the UK entity and took responsibility for the office, pension provider, health insurance, payroll and VAT returns. I also built the first front end because we did not have a front-end engineer. I knew this was part of building the company, and I was happy to take it on. Some of the work was unfamiliar, so I had to learn as I went.

As the team grew, we hired a front-end engineer and I handed that work over. That gave me more time for decisions affecting the rest of the product; I describe it in [My First Year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much). The [Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-individual-habits) chapter covers how I track work to hand over and work that comes back to me.

## Hire for the problems in front of you

Once you can describe the first work, you can make a better case for the first hires. I would start with what the team needs to be able to do and where I would be relying on knowledge I do not have.

I chose people I had worked with before. Most of the first engineers had been on my previous team. I knew their judgement and how they disagreed with me, and I trusted them to take a problem and run with it. I expected AI to multiply what a small, experienced team could do. That part held up; I wrote about it in [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

It meant paying more for experience at the beginning. My bet was that people who could frame a problem, use AI well and recognise when an approach needed changing would get useful work in front of customers sooner. That was a choice for this product and stage. I would also check whether experience in a company of hundreds fits the work we need now. Some of what made someone valuable there may matter less here, and some early-stage tasks may be new to them.

There is another assumption to check if you hire people you know: shared history does not tell them what they can decide in this company. I relied too much on trust and left too much of that arrangement unstated. I would make decision ownership part of the initial conversation; [Decisions](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits) explains what I changed later.

“Know your talent, hire your weaknesses” is a line from our values that has been useful here. I hired specialists because they knew things I did not. The process itself is in [People](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks#hiring).

## Get one useful piece into a customer's hands

You have a customer willing to work with you and people who can build. I would choose a piece of that customer's workflow we can try with them, then write down what we still do not understand. A prototype may show that a screen makes sense while telling us very little about the incoming data, the exceptions or how someone will use it alongside their existing tools.

We started with a vibe-coded prototype and tried to plan the whole product from it: modules, personas and long documents. Once design partners gave us real data, the work looked different from the tidy version we had planned. We shortened our planning cycle so we could learn and adjust sooner. [We Kept Trying to Plan Everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year) follows those changes.

I would use the unknowns to choose the next piece of discovery. If the difficult part is the data, another polished screen will not answer it. I want the engineers to see what arrives, what fails and what the customer does when it fails. A detailed specification written before that experience can make an assumption look like a settled decision.

There are still foundations I want early: ownership of services, configuration, tests and a way to release changes. I cover those in [The Engineering Foundations I Want in Place Early](/blog/category/management/setting-the-engineering-foundations). The first customer gives those choices a practical setting. We need to know who will look after the part they are using and how we will change it safely.

## Keep explaining the direction

My instinct was to set the architecture, hire people for its parts and let them get on with it. I wanted a knowledge graph at the centre, with agents, interfaces and integrations reading from and writing to that model. I laid the foundations and delegated the rest.

It worked for a while. Then decisions drifted apart, and I found out too late that some of them did not fit together. The team needed clearer priorities from me more than it needed more architecture. That is part of [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

In the scenario we started with, you will soon have new information: a partner asks for something unexpected, an integration takes longer, or two engineers make sensible choices that conflict. I would bring that information back to the people doing the work and decide with them what it changes. If it stays in founder conversations, the team keeps building against an older picture.

## Make room for the company around the product

The company will also need work from you that a product plan does not capture. I would keep a shared list of those responsibilities, with an owner for each, and review it alongside the engineering commitments. Payroll and a customer deadline can both matter in the same week; knowing that early gives the founders a chance to divide the work.

Customer requirements can add work before the product is established, too. Building for finance, we decided in the first year to pursue SOC 2 and ISO 27001. Some of the controls I wrote down described what I wanted us to have rather than what we were already doing. I discuss that gap in [Operations](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-infrastructure#compliance). I would want us to be clear about what is in place, what we have promised and who will do the remaining work.

By the end of that first planning conversation, I would want to be able to name the customer workflow we are trying, the questions it should answer, the people responsible and the company work competing for their time. We can change those answers as we go. I want the first team to have something clear enough to work from and specific enough to question.

## As the company grows

The first startup I joined hired junior people early, kept costs down and offered equity. Later it needed more experienced leadership. Some early colleagues felt that responsibilities were going to people hired above them without enough help to grow into those roles themselves. Around Series B, some started leaving. My specialist work gave me opportunities and trust that others did not always have, so I cannot treat my own experience as everyone's.

I want early employees to have a future in the company they help build. That means conversations about what they want to do next and opportunities to practise while someone can support them. If I hire an experienced manager, developing the people already there should be part of the job.

As specialists and managers take on work, I would revisit the temporary arrangements we made at the start: the responsibility, the authority that comes with it and what still returns to me. I have seen that transition in a growing company, but I have not yet made it as CTO beyond the first ten engineers.
