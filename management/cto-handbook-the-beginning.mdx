*This is a living chapter of [The Startup Handbook](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook). I edit it as our practice changes; the changelog is at the bottom.*

This chapter covers the first months: what I thought the job was, who I asked to do it with me, and the decisions that were made early because they had to be.

## Taking the role

On the first day the title meant one thing to me: when it breaks, I am the person responsible. What I had not worked out was how many different things “it” would be.

I came from research and machine learning. I had never run production infrastructure, been the name on a security policy or submitted a VAT return. A founding CTO does not get to wait for a specialist before the work is done, so the useful question early on is which parts you take yourself for now, and what would have to be true for you to hand each one over.

### How we did it at Mav9

There was no job description. There was a founding team, a product to ship, and a list of things that needed someone to pick them up, and I picked up a lot of them. I describe that list in [Nobody Handed Me a Manual](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-nobody-handed-me-a-manual).

Something I took on because nobody else could do it could stay mine long after that reason had gone. The thing to record for each one is why it is yours and what would end that. The [Individual Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-individual-habits) chapter has the two lists I now keep for this.

## The first team

My first decision was who to build with. I chose people I had worked with before. I knew how they worked and what they would say when they disagreed with me, and I could give one of them a problem and turn to something else.

There are two things to check when you hire from your own history. One is whether people who were right for a company of hundreds are right for a much earlier stage. Some of what made them valuable before matters less now, and some of what matters now they have never had to do. The other is whether you are relying on that history to carry information it cannot carry. Years of working together tell someone how I think. They do not tell them what they can decide in this company.

### How we did it at Mav9

Most of the first engineers had been on my team at my last company. I wanted experienced people who could take a problem and run with it, and I expected AI to multiply what a small team of them could do. That part held up; I wrote about it in [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

The part I got wrong was assuming trust was the whole arrangement. I had done much less to make sure they knew what they could decide and what they needed from me. The fix came later and is in [Group Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits).

One line from our values has turned out to be a good hiring rule for a CTO: know your talent, hire your weaknesses. I hired specialists because they knew things I did not. The current hiring process is in [Processes](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-processes).

## Setting a direction

Somebody has to say where the centre of the system is. Early on, you can do a lot of that through who you hire: bring in people who can each build one part of the picture, and tell them how the parts meet.

A direction set once does not keep. A customer asks for something, a part turns out harder than it looked, or two people's reasonable choices meet in the middle. Each of those needs someone to work out what it means for the rest of the plan. If nobody does, people make local decisions that are each sensible and do not always add up.

### How we did it at Mav9

I wanted a knowledge graph at the centre of the product, with the agents, interfaces and integrations reading from and writing to that one model. I hired for each part, laid the foundations and let people run with the rest.

It worked for a while. Then I found out late that some decisions did not fit together, because I had assumed the first direction would hold. The engineers needed clear priorities more than they needed more architecture from me. That story is in [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

The technical choices I wanted settled early, such as service ownership, configuration, tests and releases, are written up in [The Engineering Foundations I Want in Place Early](/blog/category/management/setting-the-engineering-foundations).

## From a prototype to real customers

A prototype is enough to start asking what needs validating. It is a weak basis for specifying every part of the finished product. The plan changes once real data arrives, so I would leave room for that from the start.

### How we did it at Mav9

We started from a vibe-coded prototype and tried to plan the whole product from it: modules, personas, long documents. It was too much. When design partners gave us real data, things looked very different from the tidy world of the prototype, and we shortened our planning cycle to learn faster. The full sequence is in [We Kept Trying to Plan Everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year).

We build for finance, so we also decided in the first year to pursue SOC 2 and ISO 27001, while the product and the team were still taking shape. Some of the controls I wrote down then described what I wanted us to have; see [Processes](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-processes).

## The work that is not engineering

A founder does whatever the company needs that nobody else is placed to do. For a CTO this can be a surprising share of the week, and it competes with the team for the same hours.

### How we did it at Mav9

The other founders are in Germany and I am in the UK. Setting up the UK entity fell to me, and so did the office, the pension provider, health insurance, payroll and VAT returns. I also built the first front end, because we had no front-end engineer.

When the admin and the team wanted the same hour, the admin usually won. I told myself that payroll going out on time was what kept the company moving. It helped, but the team needed more from me than that. When we could hire for the front end I handed it over, and that is still the clearest example I have of what a finished handover frees up. See [My First Year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much).
