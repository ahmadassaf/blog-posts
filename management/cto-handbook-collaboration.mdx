When I built the AI and research team at my first startup, I had room to choose how we worked. I pushed for us to use Notion while the wider company used Confluence and Jira. I wanted the team to be able to make practical decisions and stand behind them. That freedom mattered to me after watching the company grow and coordination become more expensive.

Several of the people I hired when starting again had worked with me before. We brought that trust with us, but we left behind the surrounding teams and established processes. I had to stop assuming that a good working relationship supplied all the context a new company needed. It did not tell someone which decision was theirs, what the founders had agreed with a customer, or whether my silence meant they should proceed.

## Meetings

A meeting should have a purpose someone can state and an output someone can find afterwards. I would keep the number small and give each one a single job, so that nobody has to work out which meeting a question belongs in.

Every meeting needs a page with its purpose, what to bring, the agenda and what comes out of it. It also needs an owner who prepares it, leads it and follows up on the decisions.

### How I do it now

| Meeting | When | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stand-up | Monday, Wednesday and Friday, 25 minutes | Keep everyone aligned on goal progress, next steps and blockers. |
| All hands | Thursday, 60 minutes | Review project progress, share shipped outcomes and company decisions, and resolve shared issues. |
| Monthly Planning | First Monday of the month, 90 minutes | Agree the project's goals and priorities and select the next goals to shape. |

Everyone attends all three.

**Stand-up.** Each person prepares a one-minute update: what moved on the goals they own or support, what they aim to move before the next stand-up, and where they need help or a decision. Goal owners update the expected date beforehand. That takes about fifteen minutes. The remaining ten are an open floor for questions and concerns. Detailed problem-solving goes to a follow-up with the people involved.

**All hands.** Ten minutes of company news from the founders, including customer wins or losses and what they mean for priorities. Fifteen minutes of shipped outcomes, where Owners demo goals that are newly done and recognise the people who helped. Fifteen minutes of milestone updates from each Lead: where it stands, what moved, the expected finish against the target date, and any risks or decisions needed. Then an open floor, and five minutes to assign anything unresolved to a named person with a date. We finish with a round of GeoGuessr.

Monthly Planning does not produce a detailed month of work. It chooses what needs shaping and who will do it. The detailed work happens afterwards between the Sponsor and the Owner, and a goal can start when its Owner has capacity. We got here after three earlier versions; see [We Kept Trying to Plan Everything](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year).

Stand-up is where the dashboard became useful to the team. It shows goal status in a conversation we were already having.

We also run retros, where people vote on suggestions for improvement. In one, someone wrote “I find planning confusing”, and it got more votes than any other suggestion.

For any meeting: show up on time or say you will be late, drop out if it is not relevant to you, and do not schedule one without an agenda and a goal. If no meeting exists for something an Owner needs to keep people informed about, the Owner sets one up.

### What happens after the call

The front-end decision taught me how easily a discussion can look settled to the person who has talked about it most. I thought I had communicated our commitment repeatedly. An engineer still could not tell that it was a company decision. Another call saying the same thing would still have left people without a record to work from.

I had proposed recording decisions where the work lives and writing up decisions made on calls that day. That habit still starts with me. The note needs to say what is decided, what remains open, who is taking the next action and when the people affected should expect an answer. I can then point to it when someone asks, and they can point to the same record when they disagree. I describe the conversations behind this in [Trust Is a System You Design](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design).

This matters when the founders are in different places too. I am in the UK and the other founders are in Germany. Someone who missed a conversation should be able to understand its consequences without waiting for the same people to be available again.

### One-to-ones

The cadence, agenda and template are in [People](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks). I wrote about moving from annual reviews to frequent check-ins in [Rethinking Performance Management](/blog/category/management/performance-management-evolution).

The employee owns the agenda, and both people protect the time. Meet at least every two weeks, and more often during onboarding or when the work calls for it. A weekly meeting is a useful habit to build. I would agree a cadence with each person and make it dependable.

For me, the reason to protect that time is what might never appear on the board. Someone may be delivering well and still feel stuck in their role. They may need help with a relationship, want feedback they have not received, or be taking on responsibility without enough support. If I spend the meeting collecting updates I could have read beforehand, those subjects have to fight for whatever time remains.

I would keep a shared note where either of us can add a topic before the meeting, then ask what most needs our attention. Make time for development and career aspirations at least monthly. I want to return to what we agreed: the introduction I said I would make, the work they wanted to try, or the concern that needed an answer. My own follow-up belongs in that note too, so they can hold me to it.

## Feedback

### Giving

Feedback is most useful early, direct and specific. Our values put it as directness with care over silence, and make conflict about facts, not people. On Slack the norm is to praise publicly and criticise privately.

When someone's decision goes badly, I first ask what they knew, and whether we had given them the context and authority we said we had. Sometimes there is a judgement to improve. Sometimes there is something I failed to provide.

Before giving feedback, identify the situation, the behaviour someone could observe and its effect. Then explain what you want to continue or change. I would check those details before the conversation. Saying someone needs to communicate better leaves them guessing which moment I mean and what I expect them to do differently.

I also need to hear their account. A late update could come from avoiding a difficult conversation, or from believing that somebody else had already passed it on. Those require different responses. I can be clear about the consequence while still being willing to correct my understanding of how it happened.

Recognition deserves the same care. I want to notice the person who made another person's delivery possible: explaining an unfamiliar service, finding a missing requirement or helping a colleague get unstuck. The visible demo rarely contains all the work that made it possible. Specific recognition helps me show which contributions I value beyond being the person presenting the result.

### Receiving

This is the half I control. The engineer who questioned the front-end commitment was challenging my account of what I had communicated. Proving how many times I had mentioned it would have missed his point, which was that it still was not clear enough to act on. How I respond affects whether he raises the next concern.

Another time, engineers picked up decisions I had left open, and I read their initiative as evidence against me. It was feedback about where I had left something unanswered. See [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

The planning retro was another check on my account of how things were going. The team had found a process confusing after we had put considerable effort into it. The amount of effort I had invested did not answer that complaint; we had to change what the planning meeting was expected to produce.

I would make room for disagreement in ordinary conversations: ask for people's views before announcing my preference, give quieter people space to finish, and acknowledge a useful challenge without making the person defend their right to raise it. I can still make a decision they disagree with. They should be able to understand why, and see what happened to the concern they brought.

### How I do it now

- The Owner and Sponsor roles create a regular place for feedback on the work: the Sponsor accepts and tests the result before it ships.
- Retro suggestions are voted on.
- The written guidance for giving feedback, and the performance review meeting, is in [People](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks).

## How the team is organised

At ten people I would organise by responsibility for an outcome. A reporting line would not tell anyone who decides what on a given goal. Titles matter less than knowing who owns a goal, who can change its scope and who has the final say in an area.

### How I do it now

We are about ten engineers. The roles people hold are attached to goals and milestones: Owner, Sponsor, Supporter and Milestone Lead. These are responsibilities people on the team take on, and the same person can be an Owner on one goal and a Supporter on another. Our first value is “we are all team leads”: the person closest to the work drives it.

The founders agree the next priorities across milestones with the Milestone Leads.

### Without a product manager

We have no product manager. The work a PM would do did not disappear: understanding customer needs, choosing what to pursue, defining outcomes, deciding what to leave out. It sits with the founders, including me, and the Sponsor role is how it reaches each goal.

I do not know yet whether we need a dedicated PM. Two things would make me revisit it: more customer requests than we can triage in a week, or finding that we keep choosing from the existing backlog because nobody has time to find out what is missing. The reasoning is in [Ten Engineers, No Product Manager](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet).

### Room to grow before there is a larger job

At my first startup, some early colleagues could not see a future for themselves as experienced leaders arrived. I understood why the company wanted that experience. I also saw people lose motivation when the business was growing and their own responsibility was not. My position was different: I had specialist expertise and room to pursue work that interested me. That made it possible to see the problem without feeling it in the same way myself.

I want to discuss that future earlier with the people I manage now. A small company cannot promise every person a management role or create a title to settle the conversation. It can give someone a meaningful responsibility, the support to attempt it and honest feedback about what they need to learn. I want incoming experience to help the people already here grow too. That is one of the lessons I took from [watching the original team change](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn#lesson-1-help-early-colleagues-grow-with-the-company).

For an engineer, the next useful responsibility might be shaping a goal with its Sponsor, helping another person operate a service, or explaining a technical tradeoff to the founders. I would choose it with them, agree what support they need and make time to discuss how it went. Completing a difficult technical task and helping other people make progress are different things to learn. Both can matter to the role they want next.

### As the company grows

We are on our fourth way of planning in a year. The latest rules are still new, and I want to find out how well they hold up before changing them again.

I have also seen what happens when a new manager brings the processes from a much larger organisation without questioning the fit. At a few hundred people, their previous approach may have solved a real coordination problem. That does not establish that a team of ten has the same problem. Before adding a meeting or a role, I want to name the decision that is being delayed, who lacks the information to make it and what the change would improve.

For a startup of ten, having everyone in the same planning and progress conversations is manageable. In a scale-up, I would move detailed updates into the teams doing the work and use shared meetings for company context and decisions that affect more than one team. Each goal would still need an Owner and a Sponsor. I would make the decisions between their areas explicit before adding another meeting to coordinate them.
