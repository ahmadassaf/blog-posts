Several people I hired had worked with me before. We brought that trust with us, but the new company needed its own shared context: which decisions were theirs, what the founders had agreed with a customer and when someone else needed to be involved.

## How product decisions reach the engineers

At ten people I would organise by responsibility for an outcome. A reporting line would not tell anyone who decides what on a given goal. Titles matter less than knowing who owns a goal, who can change its scope and who has the final say in an area.

### How I do it now

We are about ten engineers. The roles people hold are attached to goals and milestones: Owner, Sponsor, Supporter and Milestone Lead. These are responsibilities people on the team take on, and the same person can be an Owner on one goal and a Supporter on another. Our first value is “we are all team leads”: the person closest to the work drives it.

The founders agree the next priorities across milestones with the Milestone Leads.

### Without a product manager

We have no product manager. The work a PM would do did not disappear: understanding customer needs, choosing what to pursue, defining outcomes, deciding what to leave out. It sits with the founders, including me, and the Sponsor role is how it reaches each goal.

I do not know yet whether we need a dedicated PM. Two things would make me revisit it: more customer requests than we can triage in a week, or finding that we keep choosing from the existing backlog because nobody has time to find out what is missing. The reasoning is in [Ten Engineers, No Product Manager](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-building-a-team-when-there-isnt-one-yet).

## Meetings

A meeting should have a purpose someone can state and an output someone can find afterwards. I would keep the number small and give each one a single job, so that nobody has to work out which meeting a question belongs in.

Every meeting needs a page with its purpose, what to bring, the agenda and what comes out of it. It also needs an owner who prepares it, leads it and follows up on the decisions.

### How I do it now

| Meeting | When | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stand-up | Monday, Wednesday and Friday, 25 minutes | Keep everyone aligned on goal progress, next steps and blockers. |
| All hands | Thursday, 60 minutes | Review project progress, share shipped outcomes and company decisions, and resolve shared issues. |
| Monthly Planning | First Monday of the month, 90 minutes | Agree the project's goals and priorities and select the next goals to shape. |

Everyone attends all three.

**Stand-up.** Each person prepares a one-minute update: what moved on the goals they own or support, what they aim to move before the next stand-up, and where they need help or a decision. Goal owners update the expected date beforehand. That takes about fifteen minutes. The remaining ten are an open floor for questions and concerns. Detailed problem-solving goes to a follow-up with the people involved.

Stand-up is where the dashboard became useful to the team. It shows goal status in a conversation we were already having.

**All hands.** Ten minutes of company news from the founders, including customer wins or losses and what they mean for priorities. Fifteen minutes of shipped outcomes, where Owners demo goals that are newly done and recognise the people who helped. Fifteen minutes of milestone updates from each Lead: where it stands, what moved, the expected finish against the target date, and any risks or decisions needed. Then an open floor, and five minutes to assign anything unresolved to a named person with a date. We finish with a round of GeoGuessr.

[Monthly Planning](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-four-planning-processes-in-a-year) does not produce a detailed month of work. It chooses what needs shaping and who will do it. The detailed work happens afterwards between the Sponsor and the Owner, and a goal can start when its Owner has capacity.

We also run retros, where people vote on suggestions for improvement.

For any meeting: show up on time or say you will be late, drop out if it is not relevant to you, and do not schedule one without an agenda and a goal. If no meeting exists for something an Owner needs to keep people informed about, the Owner sets one up.

### What happens after the call

I had proposed [recording decisions where the work lives](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-trust-is-a-system-you-design), and writing up decisions made on calls that day. That habit still starts with me. The note needs to say what is decided, what remains open, who is taking the next action and when the people affected should expect an answer. I can then point to it when someone asks, and they can point to the same record when they disagree.

This matters when the founders are in different places too. I am in the UK and the other founders are in Germany. Someone who missed a conversation should be able to understand its consequences without waiting for the same people to be available again.

### One-to-ones

For me, the reason to protect that time is what might never appear on the board. Someone may be delivering well and still feel stuck in their role. They may need help with a relationship, want feedback they have not received, or be taking on responsibility without enough support. If I spend the meeting collecting updates I could have read beforehand, those subjects have to fight for whatever time remains.

I would agree a cadence with each person and make it dependable: at least every two weeks, and more often during onboarding or when the work calls for it. A weekly meeting is a useful habit to build. Whether that cadence is consistently in use still needs confirming.

I want to return to what we agreed: the introduction I said I would make, the work they wanted to try, or the concern that needed an answer. My own follow-up belongs in the shared note too, so they can hold me to it.

The [procedure in People](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks#one-to-ones) covers agenda ownership, shared notes and making time for development at least monthly. I wrote about moving from annual reviews to frequent check-ins in [Rethinking Performance Management](/blog/category/management/performance-management-evolution).

## When someone tells me what is missing

### The message in the team channel

An engineer once wrote to the whole channel asking us to hold ourselves to deadlines and work on one thing at a time. He wanted stand-ups to include what each person was working on and when it would be done. I read that message, and other decisions engineers picked up, as evidence against me. Their initiative was useful feedback about where I had left something unanswered. I describe that reaction in [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real).

I want people to see what happened to the concern they raised. If I still make a decision they disagree with, I owe them my reasons. I describe how I try to hear those concerns in [Decisions](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits#making-room-before-i-give-my-answer).

### Giving feedback

Feedback is most useful early, direct and specific. Our values put it as directness with care over silence, and make conflict about facts, not people. On Slack the norm is to praise publicly and criticise privately.

Before I give feedback, I would pin down when it happened, what the person did and what followed. That is the Center for Creative Leadership’s [Situation–Behavior–Impact model](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/). From there I can say what I want them to keep doing or change. Saying someone needs to communicate better leaves them guessing which moment I mean and what I expect them to do differently.

I also need to hear their account. A late update could come from avoiding a difficult conversation, or from believing that somebody else had already passed it on. Those require different responses. I can be clear about the consequence while still being willing to correct my understanding of how it happened.

Recognition deserves the same care. I want to notice the person who made another person's delivery possible: explaining an unfamiliar service, finding a missing requirement or helping a colleague get unstuck. The visible demo rarely contains all the work that made it possible. Specific recognition helps me show which contributions I value beyond being the person presenting the result.

### How I do it now

- The Owner and Sponsor roles create a regular place for feedback on the work: the Sponsor accepts and tests the result before it ships.
- Retro suggestions are voted on.
- The written guidance for giving feedback, and the performance review meeting, is in [People](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-playbooks).

## Giving people room to grow

I want to discuss people's future in the company while there is time to help them prepare. A small company cannot promise every person a management role or create a title to settle the conversation. It can give someone a meaningful responsibility, the support to attempt it and honest feedback about what they need to learn. I want incoming experience to help the people already here grow too. That is one of the lessons I took from [watching the original team change](/blog/category/management/from-employee-six-to-a-unicorn#lesson-1-help-early-colleagues-grow-with-the-company).

For an engineer, the next useful responsibility might be shaping a goal with its Sponsor, helping another person operate a service, or explaining a technical tradeoff to the founders. I would choose it with them, agree what support they need and make time to discuss how it went. Completing a difficult technical task and helping other people make progress are different things to learn. Both can matter to the role they want next.

### As the company grows

Before adding a meeting or a role, I want to name the decision that is being delayed, who lacks the information to make it and what the change would improve.

For a startup of ten, having everyone in the same planning and progress conversations is manageable. In a scale-up, I would move detailed updates into the teams doing the work and use shared meetings for company context and decisions that affect more than one team. Each goal would still need an Owner and a Sponsor. I would make the decisions between their areas explicit before adding another meeting to coordinate them.
