## SAP: Where Doing Scrum Was the Revolution

My career started at SAP, and you could not ask for a better place to learn what agile was reacting against. A traditional enterprise with decades of process in its bones: release trains measured in quarters, documents signed before code was written, and a hierarchy for every decision. In that world, simply running Scrum (actual standups, actual sprints, an actual board) felt genuinely revolutionary. It was the buzzword era everywhere, but inside a company like that, the ceremonies were not theater yet; they were rebellion. (If you're new to these concepts, I've written a [comprehensive introduction to classical Agile, Scrum, and Kanban](/blog/category/management/introduction-to-classical-agile) that covers the fundamentals.)

The appeal was obvious. Waterfall felt rigid and slow, often delivering products that were outdated by the time they launched. Agile promised flexibility, faster iterations, and better alignment with customer needs, and against a backdrop of quarterly release trains, a two-week sprint felt like teleportation.

But even then I noticed the failure mode that would follow me for years: teams adopting the rituals without the principles. Standups that ran for 30 minutes as status reports to a project manager. Sprint planning that felt like a commitment ceremony rather than collaborative planning. Retrospectives that were complaint sessions with no action items. Story points quietly becoming deadline commitments; velocity becoming a scoreboard; the backlog becoming a wishlist nobody dared to prune. In a company that size, agile theater is almost gravitational: the org chart pulls every new practice back into the shape of the old ones.

## Beamery: Six People Doing Sprints Anyway

Then I joined Beamery as an early-stage startup, and the contrast was total. We were six people. Six. And we still did agile properly: sprints, planning, standups, the whole rhythm. It sounds absurd for a team that could hold its entire coordination problem in one conversation over coffee, but it worked, because at that size the ceremonies are nearly weightless and the rhythm itself is the value: a cadence for deciding, a moment for demoing, a habit of asking what we learned.

As the company scaled, we leaned into Scrum harder rather than lighter. We brought in a scrum coach, formalized the retros, tightened the ceremonies. And for a while that was right: each layer of process was absorbing a real coordination cost that growth had created. This is the part of the journey where the textbook works: a product organization of stable teams shipping a roadmap is exactly the world Scrum was designed for.

But keep scaling and you watch Scrum decay in slow motion, the same drift I'd seen at SAP arriving from the other direction. The ceremonies that had been good practice hardened back into rituals: retros held because the calendar said so, plannings performed for the process rather than the plan. And the real bottleneck quietly moved somewhere Scrum has nothing to say about. It was no longer any team's sprint planning; it was organizational planning: how the company's bets cascade into the EPD department, how EPD's priorities cascade into engineering, and how any of it lands as coherent work across dozens of teams. Scrum is a team-sized answer, and past a certain size the hard question stops being team-sized.

## The AI Team: Planning in the Era of Uncertainty

The break came when I started the AI team at Beamery. R&D work refuses the core assumptions Scrum rests on. An estimate assumes you know what you're building; in research, half the backlog is questions ("can this model even do this?"), and you cannot put a story point on an answer you don't have. A sprint commitment assumes the goal survives two weeks; in R&D, the Tuesday experiment routinely invalidates the Monday plan. We would "fail" sprint after sprint while doing exactly what a research team should do: learning fast and changing course.

The problem wasn't the team; it was planning itself in an environment of that much uncertainty. So we stopped pretending. We kept the parts of Scrum that create feedback (the demo cadence, the retros) and dropped the parts that create fiction (sprint commitments, estimation poker for unknowable work), and ran the day-to-day as a WIP-limited Kanban flow where research spikes moved through explicit states. A mix of Scrum's rhythm and Kanban's honesty. That experience taught me to ask, for every ceremony: what feedback loop does this create, and does that loop survive contact with our uncertainty?

## Discovering Improvement Kata: A Different Lens

Then I stumbled upon improvement kata, and it fundamentally changed how I thought about continuous improvement and project management. It's a practice Mike Rother distilled from Toyota's management culture, built around a simple four-step pattern of scientific thinking: understand the direction, grasp the current condition, set the next target condition, and experiment your way toward it. I've since written a [full post on the practice](/blog/category/management/improvement-kata), so I'll keep this to what it changed for me.

What struck me was its emphasis on learning through experimentation rather than following a prescribed process. It wasn't about doing sprints or standups or any specific ceremony. Instead of asking "Are we following the Scrum guide correctly?", we started asking "What are we trying to achieve, and what experiment can we run to learn more?" The coaching kata that accompanies it (five fixed questions a coach asks, every time) created a culture where obstacles became learning opportunities rather than blockers, and teams got comfortable with uncertainty.

## Mav9: Building the Plane While Flying It

Now I've started Mav9, and I'm doing the startup-from-the-ground-up thing again. This time we don't run Scrum. Honestly, we don't run any form of proper planning, because there is nothing stable enough to plan against: we are building the airplane while flying it, and the destination updates mid-flight. A sprint commitment at this stage would be fiction with a two-week expiry date.

What survived, once again, is the combination that keeps earning its place: a lightweight Kanban flow (a board, explicit states, ruthless WIP limits) plus the [improvement kata](/blog/category/management/improvement-kata) as the steering mechanism, with target conditions doing the job sprint goals used to pretend to do. The symmetry with early Beamery is what strikes me most. Mav9 today is seven people (nine counting the founders), almost exactly the size Beamery was when we ran the full ceremony book. Beamery at that size did sprints because we didn't yet know what the ceremonies were for; Mav9 at the same size skips them because now I do, and I can keep the feedback loops without the theater.

## Where I Am Now: A Synthesis of Practices

Today, my approach to project management is a synthesis of everything that arc taught me: agile principles, improvement kata thinking, and hard-won pragmatism. I've moved away from religious adherence to any single framework and toward a more adaptive, context-sensitive approach.

Here's what this looks like in practice:

### Focus on Flow, Not Ceremonies

I care less about whether we're doing sprints "correctly" and more about whether work is flowing smoothly through our system. We identify bottlenecks, measure cycle time, and optimize for reducing work-in-progress. Some teams use sprints, some use continuous flow. The choice depends on the context, team maturity, and the nature of the work.

### Experiments Over Plans

Inspired by improvement kata, we frame everything as experiments. We might hypothesize that pairing developers will reduce bugs, so we run a time-boxed experiment and measure the results. We might think that smaller user stories lead to faster delivery, so we test it. This mindset shift from "implementing best practices" to "running experiments" has made teams more engaged and reduced the defensiveness that often comes with process changes.

### Target Conditions Over Backlogs

Rather than maintaining massive backlogs that nobody looks at, we define clear target conditions for what we want to achieve. This could be "reduce deployment time to under 10 minutes" or "achieve 90% test coverage in the authentication module." The work we do is in service of moving toward these target conditions, and we're ruthless about saying no to anything that doesn't clearly contribute.

### Regular Reflection, Flexible Format

We've kept the retrospective spirit but made the format flexible. Sometimes it's a structured retro, sometimes it's a group problem-solving session, sometimes it's a celebration of wins. The key is that we regularly pause to reflect and adjust. The improvement kata coaching questions often guide these sessions.

### Metrics That Matter

We've stopped using velocity or story points as productivity scores; [used as a team's private forecasting input](/blog/category/management/measuring-agile-teams), velocity still earns its keep, but as a scoreboard it's vanity. Instead, we focus on outcomes: customer satisfaction, deployment frequency, mean time to recovery, and cycle time. These metrics tell us whether we're actually improving our ability to deliver value, not just whether we're "busy."

### Psychological Safety as Foundation

None of this works without psychological safety. Teams need to feel safe experimenting, admitting uncertainty, and discussing obstacles openly. Building this safety has become a primary focus, more important than any process or tool.

## The Journey Continues

The evolution of my project management approach reflects a broader maturity in how I think about process and improvement. At SAP I was looking for the "right way" to do things, and Scrum looked like it. At Beamery I watched the right way work, then scale, then stop fitting. At Mav9 I finally stopped looking for a right way at all.

Now I understand that there is no "right way", only practices that work better or worse in specific contexts. The goal isn't to follow a methodology perfectly, but to build teams that can continuously learn and adapt. Agile gave us permission to iterate quickly. Improvement kata gave us a systematic way to learn from those iterations. What I do now is an ongoing experiment in combining these ideas with the realities of the teams and contexts I work in.

The only constant is the commitment to keep learning, keep experimenting, and keep adapting. Everything else in this post is subject to the next retro.
