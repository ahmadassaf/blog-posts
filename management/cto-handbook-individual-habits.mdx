These are the habits I use to run my own week, along with a few I want to make more dependable. Most came from a specific thing going wrong.

## Notice when your job has changed

At the first startup, I had work I understood well and room to build a specialist team. I knew where people were stuck and how their work connected. Starting again changed the work around me much faster than it changed what I reached for each morning. I took on the front end because we had nobody else to build it, alongside setting up the company in the UK.

Both helped us get started. Later, those responsibilities still occupied me while the engineers waited for clearer priorities. My commit history showed plenty of technical involvement. It did not show that I was losing my understanding of how the rest of the system fitted together, or that I held customer context the team needed.

Being busy was a poor way to judge whether I was doing the work the company now needed from me. The useful clues were more specific: a responsibility I still held after we could give it to someone else, or a decision an engineer had to make because I had left it open. [My first year as CTO](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-the-cto-who-does-too-much) gave me plenty of those clues.

I want to notice a change in the job before waiting for frustration to make it obvious. For someone early in a startup, that may mean learning to lead people they used to work alongside. For me, it meant taking the wider product and company responsibilities seriously enough to make time for them, while staying close enough to engineering to be useful.

## Keep lists of what actually happened

A general feeling of being behind is hard to act on. A list of specific entries is not. When I write down what went wrong, most entries turn out to be small enough to do something about this week.

### How I do it

I keep three lists.

- **Work I should hand over.** Things I took on because nobody else could at the time. Each one needs a person who can own it, with the access, context and authority to do so.
- **Work that came back to me.** When something I handed over returns, I write it down and ask what was missing from the handover: a person, access, context, authority or time to do it properly.
- **Things I got wrong and gaps I found.** A missing decision becomes a conversation to have this week. A missing fact becomes a note.

Reading the first two together is how I notice that a temporary arrangement has become permanent. The third is shorter than the voice in my head suggests; I wrote about that voice in [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real). To sort my commitments in the first place I used the exercise in Lara Hogan's [When to delegate, when to say no](https://larahogan.me/blog/when-to-delegate-when-to-say-no/).

The front end was something I could hand over once we hired for it. Product work was different: we still had no product manager, so the founders needed to make their responsibilities clearer. I would handle an entry in the first list differently depending on which situation it describes. Sometimes I need a person. Sometimes I need to give the existing owner the missing authority or context. Writing “delegate” beside both would hide the decision I still had to make.

## The second time, write it down

If I do a piece of work twice, the third time should start from instructions. Writing them takes longer than doing the work once more. It pays back when someone else can follow them, including an AI assistant, and when the task stops depending on my memory of how it went last time.

### How I do it

When work repeats, I write down what to look at, what to do with it, and which decisions need to come back to me. An assistant can follow that procedure and I can inspect the result. The same document makes the task easier to hand to a person.

The same applies to things I say. I once felt as though I had announced a decision in every call for a month, and an engineer told me it had never been formally communicated. Saying something repeatedly is a sign it needs to be written where people can find it. More on that in [Group Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits).

I would write the instructions with the next person in mind. They need enough context to know when the procedure applies and when to stop and ask. A list of steps that only works because I remember the exceptions is not ready to hand over. When a task comes back, I can use the gap to improve the instructions instead of expecting the next person to infer what I left out.

## Close the loop on what you promised

If I promise an answer, I need to come back with it, or say why I cannot yet and when I will. The same applies when I decide not to act on a concern. An unanswered question leaves someone guessing whether I heard it at all, and they are less likely to raise the next one.

### How we do it at Mav9

In a draft on product ownership I proposed a response time of one working day for a scope question, a concern or a draft. In return, the engineer brings what they understand, what is missing and a recommendation. It is a proposal the team can challenge, and I have to make the time to keep my side of it. Asking people to raise uncertainty early makes little sense if their questions then sit unanswered.

An answer does not always mean the decision is ready. I can explain what I am waiting for and when I will return to it. That gives the other person something to plan around.

## Arrive prepared, then pay attention

The Management Playbook sets out a useful discipline for 1:1s: keep a shared agenda, let the employee's concerns shape the conversation, and refer back to what was agreed. It also asks the manager to be punctual and give the person their attention. Those are small obligations, but they make the scheduled time usable.

I would start by reading the previous notes before the meeting. If I promised to find information, arrange access or make a decision, I should bring the answer or explain what happened. The other person should not need to use their time reminding me of my own commitment. Then I want enough room for what has changed since the agenda was written.

For a CTO, there is a particular temptation to take a conversation straight into solving the technical problem. The playbook's suggestion to coach before supplying the solution is useful here. I would ask what the person has tried, where they need a decision and what help they want from me. If the issue is missing authority or an unanswered product question, another implementation idea will not resolve it.

I would finish with a short record of what each of us will do and when we will return to it. This is the practice I want to make dependable. The wider purpose and structure of 1:1s belong in [Collaboration](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-collaboration). Here, the habit is doing my preparation and following through afterwards.

## Ask for help with what you do not know

A CTO is responsible for decisions across subjects they are not expert in. I need enough understanding to question an approach, see where it affects someone else's work and recognise when I need help. Trying to be equally good at every specialist's job would leave less time for mine.

### How I did it at Mav9

I told the team that I had been further from the implementation than I wanted to be and did not have the end-to-end understanding many of them had. Then I asked each person to write down how their part of the system worked. Those explanations were the start of the wider picture coming back, and saying it out loud helped quiet the voice that told me I should already know.

I need to keep learning separate from taking work back. I needed to understand how the parts connected so I could help with decisions affecting the whole product. The engineers still owned their parts. Asking them to explain a choice did not mean I should become its approver or reproduce their expertise before they could continue.

I value time to explore because it has been a large part of the work I enjoy. Trust made that possible in the team I built before, and it is one reason I chose to work with many of those people again. I owe them the same room now. When I need help understanding the system, I can ask for an explanation without treating every hour spent investigating something unfamiliar as a delivery problem.

## Use an assistant for the reading around a decision

The job keeps pulling me into subjects I do not know well. Before a decision I need to understand what already exists, where the relevant information is and which question is still open. An assistant is good at bringing that together, provided I can check it.

### How I do it

I ask for links back to the files or records it used, so I can open the source behind a claim. I ask it to explain the part I do not understand, and sometimes to challenge an assumption while I am still working out what I think. Deciding whether the result does the job stays with me: whether the explanation holds up against its sources, or the fix addresses the problem. More in [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

The same care is needed when the assistant helps me write something for the team. We made our early product documents longer with AI, until they were hard to read. I need to read the result before asking anyone else to spend time on it, check the claims and remove the explanation that does not help them act. If the draft makes an unresolved question sound decided, editing it means restoring the uncertainty. A polished answer is a poor substitute for telling the team what is still open.

I also need to be able to explain the diagrams and recommendations I share. That is a useful test of whether the assistant has helped me understand the work or merely given me something plausible to circulate. The next person should be able to find the decision and its reasoning without having to work through the whole conversation that produced it.

## Protect time for the work only you can do

Technical work is easy to reach for. I can close a ticket and know I did something. It is harder to tell whether I gave someone enough context or made a good hiring decision, and that kind of work gets pushed out unless it has time of its own.

### How I do it

Hiring, difficult tradeoffs and questions that cross responsibilities need time of their own in my week. I still build things, and sometimes that is the most useful contribution I can make. I try to notice when building has become the thing I do while a harder decision waits.

On the company side, our norms help: we set a Slack status for focus time, and a rule of thumb is that if you do not expect to respond within about an hour, you say so.

## Write in public

I write to work out what I think and to give someone else the chance to correct me. Writing about a mistake makes me explain what I misread. See [Why I Write](/blog/category/thoughts/why-i-write) and [Learning in Public](/blog/category/thoughts/learning-in-public).
