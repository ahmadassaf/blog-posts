*This is a living chapter of [The Startup Handbook](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook). I edit it as our practice changes; the changelog is at the bottom.*

These are the habits I use to run my own week. None of them is a system. Most came from a specific thing going wrong.

## Keep lists of what actually happened

A general feeling of being behind is hard to act on. A list of specific entries is not. When I write down what went wrong, most entries turn out to be small enough to do something about this week.

### How I do it

I keep three lists.

- **Work I should hand over.** Things I took on because nobody else could at the time. Each one needs a person who can own it, with the access, context and authority to do so.
- **Work that came back to me.** When something I handed over returns, I write it down and ask what was missing from the handover: a person, access, context, authority or time to do it properly.
- **Things I got wrong and gaps I found.** A missing decision becomes a conversation to have this week. A missing fact becomes a note.

Reading the first two together is how I notice that a temporary arrangement has become permanent. The third is shorter than the voice in my head suggests; I wrote about that voice in [Imposter Syndrome Is Real](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-imposter-syndrome-is-real). To sort my commitments in the first place I used the exercise in Lara Hogan's [When to delegate, when to say no](https://larahogan.me/blog/when-to-delegate-when-to-say-no/).

## The second time, write it down

If I do a piece of work twice, the third time should start from instructions. Writing them takes longer than doing the work once more. It pays back when someone else can follow them, including an AI assistant, and when the task stops depending on my memory of how it went last time.

### How I do it

When work repeats, I write down what to look at, what to do with it, and which decisions need to come back to me. An assistant can follow that procedure and I can inspect the result. The same document makes the task easier to hand to a person.

The same applies to things I say. I once felt as though I had announced a decision in every call for a month, and an engineer told me it had never been formally communicated. Saying something repeatedly is a sign it needs to be written where people can find it. More on that in [Group Habits](/blog/category/management/cto-handbook-group-habits).

## Close the loop on what you promised

If I promise an answer, I need to come back with it, or say why I cannot yet and when I will. The same applies when I decide not to act on a concern. An unanswered question leaves someone guessing whether I heard it at all, and they are less likely to raise the next one.

### How we do it at Mav9

In a draft on product ownership I proposed a response time of one working day for a scope question, a concern or a draft. In return, the engineer brings what they understand, what is missing and a recommendation. It is a proposal the team can challenge, and I have to make the time to keep my side of it. Asking people to raise uncertainty early makes little sense if their questions then sit unanswered.

## Ask for help with what you do not know

A CTO is responsible for decisions across subjects they are not expert in. I need enough understanding to question an approach, see where it affects someone else's work and recognise when I need help. Trying to be equally good at every specialist's job would leave less time for mine.

### How I did it at Mav9

I told the team that I had been further from the implementation than I wanted to be and did not have the end-to-end understanding many of them had. Then I asked each person to write down how their part of the system worked. Those explanations were the start of the wider picture coming back, and saying it out loud helped quiet the voice that told me I should already know.

## Use an assistant for the reading around a decision

The job keeps pulling me into subjects I do not know well. Before a decision I need to understand what already exists, where the relevant information is and which question is still open. An assistant is good at bringing that together, provided I can check it.

### How I do it

I ask for links back to the files or records it used, so I can open the source behind a claim. I ask it to explain the part I do not understand, and sometimes to challenge an assumption while I am still working out what I think. Deciding whether the result does the job stays with me: whether the explanation holds up against its sources, or the fix addresses the problem. More in [AI Is a Multiplier, Not a Signature](/blog/category/management/becoming-a-cto-ai-is-a-multiplier-not-a-signature).

## Protect time for the work only you can do

Technical work is easy to reach for. I can close a ticket and know I did something. It is harder to tell whether I gave someone enough context or made a good hiring decision, and that kind of work gets pushed out unless it has time of its own.

### How I do it

Hiring, difficult tradeoffs and questions that cross responsibilities need time of their own in my week. I still build things, and sometimes that is the most useful contribution I can make. I try to notice when building has become the thing I do while a harder decision waits.

On the company side, our norms help: we set a Slack status for focus time, and a rule of thumb is that if you do not expect to respond within about an hour, you say so.

## Write in public

I write to work out what I think and to give someone else the chance to correct me. Writing about a mistake makes me explain what I misread. See [Why I Write](/blog/category/thoughts/why-i-write) and [Learning in Public](/blog/category/thoughts/learning-in-public).
